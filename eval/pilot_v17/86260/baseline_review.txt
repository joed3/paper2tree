**eLife Assessment**
This potentially useful study describes metabolic differences between endothelial cells isolated from coronary thrombectomy specimens of AMI patients and a single commercial healthy coronary endothelial cell line, but the central claims of cell-autonomous metabolic dysfunction rest on incomplete evidence owing to fundamental limitations in experimental controls, the absence of direct metabolic flux measurements, and a lack of functional/interventional experiments to establish causality.

**Summary**
The authors isolate endothelial cells from coronary atherothrombotic specimens of eight acute myocardial infarction (AMI) patients and compare them to a commercially purchased human coronary artery endothelial cell line (HCAEC). They report that AMI-derived endothelial cells (AMIECs) display reduced growth rates, impaired migration, and diminished tubulogenesis. Metabolically, AMIECs are described as having lower glucose consumption but paradoxically high lactate production, downregulated PFKFB3 and upregulated PFKFB4 (suggesting a glycolysis-to-pentose phosphate pathway shunt), elevated ROS countered by increased glutathione, enhanced glutamine consumption and glutaminase expression, and hyperpolarized mitochondrial membranes. The authors synthesize these observations into a model proposing two primary cell-autonomous deficiencies: (1) strong proton coupling in mitochondria generating excess ROS, and (2) a carbon source switch from glucose to glutamine. They conclude these represent "pharmacologically actionable" targets, though no pharmacological interventions are tested.

**Strengths**
- The use of patient-derived coronary endothelial cells from thrombectomy specimens is a creative and clinically relevant approach that provides access to disease-relevant cell material that is otherwise difficult to obtain.
- The study examines multiple interconnected metabolic pathways (glycolysis, PPP, glutaminolysis, redox homeostasis) and attempts to construct an integrated metabolic model, rather than focusing on a single pathway in isolation.
- The consistency of phenotypes across most of the eight patient-derived cell lines (with CE-45 as a notable and informative outlier) lends some support to the generalizability of the observations.
- The correlation between migration capacity and tubulogenesis (Figure 2C) is a useful analysis that links functional endothelial defects.

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**

- **Inadequate control design:** The most critical limitation is the use of a single commercially purchased HCAEC line as the sole control. This conflates numerous confounders—different donors, isolation procedures, passage histories, genetic backgrounds, and potentially donor age/sex—with disease-related differences. Multiple independent healthy coronary endothelial cell preparations, ideally processed through comparable isolation protocols, are essential. Without this, none of the observed differences can be confidently attributed to AMI-related pathology.

- **No direct metabolic flux measurements:** The central metabolic claims (glycolysis-to-PPP shunt, glutamine as alternative carbon source, glutamine-derived lactate) are inferred from steady-state enzyme expression levels and extracellular metabolite concentrations. Stable isotope tracing (e.g., ¹³C-glucose, ¹³C-glutamine) is required to directly demonstrate the proposed flux rearrangements. Similarly, Seahorse extracellular flux analysis (or equivalent respirometry) is needed to substantiate claims about mitochondrial respiration rates.

- **No functional/interventional experiments:** Despite framing these findings as "pharmacologically actionable," the paper tests no pharmacological interventions (e.g., mitochondrial uncouplers, glutaminase inhibitors, PFKFB3 activators) to determine whether reversing the metabolic phenotype rescues functional defects. This severely limits the translational significance claimed.

- **Cellular senescence not excluded:** The slow growth, impaired migration, and elevated ROS could reflect cellular senescence rather than a specific metabolic reprogramming. Senescence markers (SA-β-galactosidase, p16^INK4a^, p21, SASP factors) must be assessed, particularly given that these cells originate from diseased atherosclerotic tissue.

- **Mitochondrial membrane potential measurement is ambiguous:** Increased DiIC1(5) accumulation could reflect increased mitochondrial mass (more mitochondria per cell) rather than increased potential per mitochondrion. Mitochondrial mass should be independently quantified (e.g., MitoTracker Green, citrate synthase activity, mtDNA copy number), and a ratiometric dye such as JC-1 would strengthen membrane potential conclusions.

- **ROS measurement limitations:** H2DCFDA is notoriously non-specific and susceptible to artifacts from differences in esterase activity, dye loading, and efflux between cell types. More specific ROS probes (MitoSOX for mitochondrial superoxide, Amplex Red for H₂O₂) and/or enzymatic assays are needed to validate this finding and localize ROS production to mitochondria as claimed.

- **Statistical framework is problematic:** Student's t-tests comparing each individual AMIEC line (n=3 technical replicates) against the single HCAEC line do not account for biological variability among controls. The appropriate statistical framework should treat the eight patient lines and multiple control lines as biological replicates and use methods that account for inter-individual variation.

- **Passage number and culture duration effects:** The paper does not report passage numbers for AMIECs vs. HCAECs, nor whether phenotypes are stable across passages. Given the hypothesis of epigenetic memory, serial passage experiments would be informative and are necessary to distinguish stable cell-autonomous features from transient culture adaptation.

- **Causal direction not established:** The paper does not distinguish whether metabolic alterations drive endothelial dysfunction or are consequences of it. Even correlative evidence (e.g., does the degree of metabolic deviation predict the severity of functional impairment across the eight lines?) is not systematically explored beyond the migration-tubulogenesis correlation.

- **Key mechanistic claims are speculative:** The proposed glutamine-to-lactate pathway via malic enzyme and LDH-A, the hypothesis involving p53 loss of function, and the model of proton coupling as the primary defect are presented without supporting experimental evidence. These require either direct testing or should be clearly delineated as speculation.

**Minor Comments**

- Figure legend panel labels are inconsistent with the text (e.g., Figure 1 legend describes panels A–G but the text references them differently; growth rate appears labeled as 1D in legend but 1C in text context). This needs careful reconciliation.
- The manuscript would benefit from a comprehensive supplementary table showing all patient clinical data alongside the measured cellular/metabolic parameters, enabling readers to assess potential correlations with clinical features (e.g., diabetes status, statin use).
- The discussion of PFKFB3/PFKFB4 regulation by HIF-1α, mTOR, PPARγ, and p53 is interesting but entirely speculative in this context. HIF-1α levels, p53 status, and mTOR activity should either be measured or this section substantially shortened.
- The GC/MS metabolite data (Figure 5E) would benefit from proper multivariate analysis (PCA or similar) and presentation of individual metabolite levels with appropriate statistics.
- The methods section states "detailed protocols are available upon request," which does not meet eLife's transparency standards; full protocols should be provided or deposited in a public repository.
- Raw data for Western blots, flow cytometry, and metabolite measurements should be deposited in an appropriate public repository.
- The claim that CE-45 behaves as a "normal-like" outlier is intriguing but not explored—are there distinguishing clinical features for this patient?

**Recommendation**
Major Revisions
The patient-derived endothelial cell model is a valuable resource, and the integrated metabolic characterization is conceptually interesting, but the study in its current form does not meet eLife's evidentiary standards due to inadequate controls, absence of direct metabolic flux measurements, lack of functional rescue experiments, and failure to exclude alternative explanations such as senescence—revisions addressing these fundamental issues are required before the central claims can be considered supported.
