**eLife Assessment**

This study presents a **useful** characterization of metabolic alterations in endothelial cells derived from acute myocardial infarction thrombectomy specimens, but the evidence supporting the central mechanistic claims is **incomplete**. While multiple metabolic parameters are measured across eight patient-derived cell lines with some internally consistent patterns, the reliance on a single commercial control cell line, the absence of direct flux measurements, and several speculative mechanistic claims that lack experimental support prevent the conclusions from reaching the level of rigor expected for publication in eLife.

**Summary**

The authors isolate endothelial cells from thrombectomy specimens of eight AMI patients (AMIECs) and compare them to a single commercially obtained human coronary artery endothelial cell line (HCAEC). They report that AMIECs exhibit impaired growth, migration, and tubulogenesis, alongside a constellation of metabolic alterations: reduced glucose consumption with paradoxically high lactate production, downregulation of PFKFB3 and upregulation of PFKFB4, enhanced G6PD expression and activity, elevated ROS and glutathione, increased glutamine consumption and GLS1 expression, and mitochondrial hyperpolarization. From these observations, the authors construct a model in which AMIECs undergo a cell-autonomous metabolic switch from glycolysis to glutaminolysis, with compensatory pentose phosphate pathway activation to generate NADPH for glutathione-mediated ROS detoxification, and propose that pharmacological mitochondrial uncoupling could be a therapeutic strategy.

The study addresses a genuinely important question—whether coronary endothelial cells from AMI patients harbor intrinsic, cell-autonomous metabolic dysfunction—and the approach of deriving endothelial cells directly from patient thrombectomy specimens is creative. However, the central mechanistic model is largely inferential, with each step in the proposed pathway inferred from expression data or metabolite levels rather than directly measured by flux analysis, and several key claims remain unsupported by experimental evidence.

**Strengths**

- **Endothelial identity confirmation (high support):** Four endothelial markers (V-CAM1, PECAM-1, CD-34, CD-105) are verified by Western blot with source data provided, establishing that the isolated cells are indeed endothelial in nature.

- **Consistent PFKFB3 downregulation (high support):** Seven of eight AMIECs show significantly lower PFKFB3 protein levels by triplicate Western blot with β-actin normalization, and the exception (CE-45) corresponds to the cell line with near-normal glucose consumption, providing internal mechanistic consistency.

- **G6PD protein expression and enzyme activity (high support):** Both protein levels and direct enzyme activity are measured, providing functional rather than purely correlational evidence for PPP engagement—a notable strength relative to other claims that rely solely on expression data.

- **ROS and glutathione co-elevation (high support):** Seven of eight AMIECs show elevated ROS and all eight show elevated glutathione, with CE-45's near-normal ROS and G6PD profile serving as a compelling internal consistency check across multiple assays.

- **GLS1 isoform expression (high support):** Both KGA and GAC isoforms are measured with isoform-specific antibodies, and the dissociation between high lactate production and low glucose consumption (high support) is a striking and consistently observed metabolic feature across six of eight lines.

- **Internal consistency of CE-45 (high support across multiple evidence nodes):** CE-45's co-variation across glucose consumption, PFKFB3 levels, ROS levels, G6PD expression, and amino acid profiles provides a within-study validation of the overall metabolic narrative.

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**

- **Single control cell line (critical flaw affecting all claims):** Every comparison in the paper is made against a single commercially purchased HCAEC line. Without multiple healthy donor-derived coronary endothelial cell controls, it is impossible to distinguish AMI-specific metabolic alterations from normal inter-individual variation or lot-specific features of this particular control. This is flagged as a required assumption underlying the root claim and every primary claim. At minimum, three to five independent healthy donor controls are needed for any generalizable conclusions.

- **Glutamine-derived lactate hypothesis (low support):** The claim that high lactate production despite low glucose consumption is explained by glutamine-to-pyruvate-to-lactate conversion is entirely speculative. No ¹³C-glutamine tracing is performed, no malic enzyme or LDH-A expression/activity is measured, and no stoichiometric analysis assesses whether glutamine consumption rates could quantitatively account for observed lactate production. The culture medium likely contains pyruvate and other carbon sources that could explain this observation. This claim requires isotope tracing experiments to be credible.

- **Pharmacological uncoupling as therapy (low support):** The claim that mitochondrial uncoupling is "pharmacologically actionable" appears in the abstract but no uncoupler is tested in the study. No rescue experiment demonstrates that uncoupling reverses any phenotype. The cited UCP1 knockout study (Gu et al., 2021) is from an animal model involving a gene primarily expressed in brown adipose tissue, not endothelial cells. This claim should either be removed or substantially downgraded to a speculative discussion point.

- **NAD+ depletion model (low support):** The claim that pyruvate-to-lactate conversion serves to replenish NAD+ is presented without any NAD+ or NADH measurements. Furthermore, the mechanistic reasoning is questionable: if mitochondrial membrane potential is high due to active electron transport (as the authors propose), this would *increase* NAD+ regeneration, not impair it. The contradiction between "robust proton coupling" driving high membrane potential and "impaired NAD+ regeneration" is not resolved.

- **Mitochondrial ROS attribution without mitochondria-specific probes (medium support, critical weakness):** H₂DCFDA measures total cellular ROS nonspecifically. The authors acknowledge that NAD(P)H oxidases could contribute, yet the entire model attributes ROS to mitochondria. MitoSOX or other mitochondria-targeted probes are essential to support this claim. Additionally, DiIC1(5) accumulation is not normalized for mitochondrial mass (e.g., MitoTracker Green, citrate synthase activity, mtDNA copy number), so "hyperpolarization" cannot be distinguished from increased mitochondrial biogenesis.

- **No direct flux measurements for core metabolic claims:** The "glycolysis-to-PPP shunt" (medium support) is inferred from enzyme expression without any ¹³C-glucose tracing, F-2,6-BP measurements, or NADPH/NADP⁺ ratios. The "carbon source switch from glucose to glutamine" (medium support) is inferred from consumption ratios without ¹³C-glutamine tracing into TCA cycle intermediates. These are the two central metabolic claims of the paper and both require direct flux evidence.

- **Origin of metabolic alterations (low support):** The suggestion that metabolic alterations originate from "primary mitochondrial dysfunction or persistent epigenetic imprints" is presented without any genomic, mitochondrial DNA, or epigenetic analysis. No experiment distinguishes these possibilities from isolation/culture artifacts. The term "cell-autonomous" is used throughout without formal demonstration (e.g., conditioned media experiments, co-culture rescue, or passage stability analysis).

- **Mitomycin C concentration concern:** The stated concentration of 1 mg/mL for mitomycin C in migration assays is approximately 100-fold higher than standard concentrations (1–10 µg/mL). If accurate, differential cytotoxicity between stressed AMIECs and healthy HCAECs could confound migration results. The authors should clarify whether this is a typographical error.

**Minor Comments**

- The paper contains a biochemical error conflating NADH and NADPH: it states that glutamate dehydrogenase converts "NAD(P)⁺ into NADPH" but then claims "NADH is the substrate of complex I." These are metabolically distinct cofactors with different mitochondrial roles, and the distinction matters for the proposed model.

- The R² of 0.67 for the migration–tubulogenesis correlation is computed from only 9 data points including the control, which is likely an extreme outlier driving the regression. The authors should report the p-value and show the correlation among AMIECs alone.

- Several amino acids (glycine, alanine, histidine, proline) are exceptions to the "elevated intracellular amino acid" trend but are not discussed. Glycine is a glutathione precursor, and its non-elevation complicates the glutathione biosynthesis narrative.

- Normalization methods for glucose consumption, lactate production, and glutamine consumption rates (per cell, per protein, per well) are not clearly stated. Given the different growth rates, this is essential methodological information.

- The heterogeneity among AMIECs (CE-45 and CE-61 as metabolic outliers) warrants more systematic analysis—clustering or principal component analysis of the metabolic parameters could reveal whether the eight lines truly represent a single disease phenotype or multiple subtypes.

**Recommendation**

**Major Revisions**

While the study addresses an important question with a creative experimental approach and presents some well-supported observations (18 of 43 claims rated high support), six claims receive low support—including two primary mechanistic claims (glutamine-derived lactate, pharmacological uncoupling) and two supporting claims that underpin the central model. The critical absence of multiple healthy donor controls, direct metabolic flux measurements, and mitochondria-specific ROS probes means the central mechanistic narrative remains inferential rather than demonstrated, falling short of eLife's standard for convincing evidence supporting conceptual claims of this scope.
