**eLife Assessment**

This study presents a valuable contribution to our understanding of mammalian metabolic evolution by documenting the independent loss of the ketogenesis gene HMGCS2 in three placental mammalian lineages. The genomic evidence for gene loss is convincing, supported by multiple orthogonal validation strategies; however, the broader physiological and evolutionary claims — particularly regarding alternative metabolic strategies and the relationship between ketogenesis and brain evolution — rest on incomplete evidence that does not fully support the paper's strongest conclusions.

**Summary**

Jebb and Hiller investigate the evolution of HMGCS2, the rate-limiting enzyme in ketone body biosynthesis, across 70 placental mammalian genomes. They report that HMGCS2 has been independently lost in three lineages: cetaceans (dolphins and whales), Elephantimorpha (elephants and mastodons), and pteropodid bats (Old World fruit bats). Using shared inactivating mutations, molecular dating, and fossil encephalization quotient (EQ) data, the authors argue that HMGCS2 loss preceded brain size expansion in cetaceans and elephants, thereby challenging the hypothesis that ketogenesis was a universal precondition for the evolution of large mammalian brains. They additionally document correlated loss of the non-pleiotropic downstream gene BDH1 in two of the three lineages and propose that alternative metabolic strategies for fueling large brains during fasting evolved independently in cetaceans and elephants. The paper combines computational genomics, molecular evolution, and comparative physiology to build its argument, drawing on published physiological data for dolphins, fruit bats, and elephants.

**Strengths**

- **Genomic evidence for HMGCS2 loss is robust (claim: HMGCS2 has been independently lost in three mammalian lineages).** Multiple classes of inactivating mutations are documented (exon deletions, frameshifts, stop codons, splice site mutations), all validated by sequencing reads (≥30 reads for cetaceans with no ancestral allele support). Shared mutations across species within each lineage (e.g., shared exon 1 deletion with identical breakpoints in toothed and baleen whales) provide strong parsimony-based evidence for ancestral loss events. The inclusion of mastodon ancient DNA extends validation to ~24–28 Mya.

- **Orthology is rigorously confirmed (claim: HMGCS2 remnants are in conserved genomic context).** Synteny with REG4 and PHGDH is documented across all three lineages, and the HMGCS1 paralog is clearly distinguished. The PacBio assembly validation for the Egyptian fruit bat provides assembly-technology-independent confirmation of the complete deletion.

- **Strong purifying selection in other mammals highlights the significance of the losses (claim: HMGCS2 has dN/dS of 0.16 across other mammals).** The finding that 59/62 assessable mammals retain intact HMGCS2 under strong purifying selection effectively contextualizes how unusual these three losses are and supports the inference that ketogenesis is generally important.

- **The correlated loss of BDH1 but not pleiotropic genes is a clean evolutionary prediction fulfilled (claim: loss is associated with loss of non-pleiotropic but not pleiotropic genes).** The retention of ACAT1 and HMGCL (which serve dual roles in amino acid metabolism) alongside loss of BDH1 in cetaceans and pteropodids is consistent with evolutionary theory and strengthens the narrative, though the elephant exception is notable.

- **Computational exclusion of HMGCS1 compensation is thorough (claim: HMGCS1 cannot compensate).** TargetP analysis across all loss lineages, Augustus gene predictions, and liver RNA-seq analysis in minke whale and Egyptian fruit bat collectively argue against HMGCS1-mediated mitochondrial ketogenesis as a compensatory mechanism.

- **The cetacean temporal ordering is well-supported (claim: shared mutations show HMGCS2 loss before toothed/baleen whale split ~36 Mya).** Multiple shared inactivating mutations in exons 1, 2, and 8 provide strong evidence that HMGCS2 was already non-functional before the divergence of toothed and baleen whales, while major dolphin brain expansion clearly occurred later (EQ 1.4 → 3.7).

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**

- **The claim that "alternative metabolic strategies to fuel large brains during fasting have evolved at least twice independently" (claim c1.6) has low support and should be substantially revised.** The paper provides no direct evidence for what these alternative strategies are. For dolphins, the Ridgway (2013) data showing maintenance of blood glucose without ketone bodies during a 3-day fast is suggestive but comes from a single study in one species, and the gluconeogenesis explanation is speculative. For elephants, no metabolic data exist at all. For fruit bats, the evidence directly contradicts this claim — they die when fasted >24 hours, demonstrating that no effective alternative strategy evolved in this lineage. The authors should either provide direct metabolic evidence or substantially temper this claim, perhaps reframing it as a hypothesis requiring future experimental validation.

- **The elephant physiological evidence is inadequate (claim: elephant blood becomes alkaline during musth-associated fasting).** Blood pH during musth is influenced by numerous confounding factors including respiratory changes during aggressive behavior, massive hormonal shifts, altered renal function, and potential water loss. No ketone body measurements were performed. This single indirect observation from Rasmussen and Perrin (1999) cannot meaningfully support the claim that elephants have lost ketogenesis at the physiological level. The authors should either acknowledge this evidence as insufficient or, ideally, identify published or obtainable metabolic data from elephants during fasting.

- **The claim that these findings "demonstrate" ketone bodies are not strictly required to fuel large brains during fasting (claim c1.3) overstates the evidence.** The paper shows co-occurrence of HMGCS2 loss and large brains, but the fruit bat case (death upon fasting) actually shows that without ketogenesis, brain fueling during fasting *does* fail in at least one lineage. The "demonstration" would require showing that cetacean and elephant brains are actually metabolically sustained during fasting without ketone bodies, which is not done. The authors should weaken the language from "demonstrating" to "suggesting" or "consistent with."

- **The inference of complete loss of ketogenesis (claim c1.2) requires additional caveats.** The paper assumes HMGCS2 is the sole route to ketogenesis but does not systematically screen for alternative ketogenic pathways (e.g., gut microbiome-derived ketone bodies, non-canonical enzymatic pathways). Liver RNA-seq is available for only two of the three lineages, and the physiological conditions under which this RNA-seq was collected (fed vs. fasted state) are not discussed. The authors should explicitly acknowledge these limitations and discuss whether alternative low-level ketogenesis could persist.

- **The molecular dating for elephants (claim: HMGCS2 loss at 45–42 Mya, brain expansion at 37–27 Mya) may not establish non-overlapping temporal windows given inherent uncertainties.** The dating method assumes a fixed synonymous rate ratio (70%), and confidence intervals are not formally reported or compared. Furthermore, exact EQ values for Palaeomastodons are unknown, as the authors themselves acknowledge. The authors should provide confidence intervals for the molecular dating estimates and discuss whether the temporal gap between gene loss and brain expansion survives uncertainty analysis.

- **The paper's framing of contradicting the Cunnane and Crawford (2003) hypothesis (claim c1.3.5) may attack a straw man.** The original hypothesis concerned human evolution specifically. The authors should clarify whether Cunnane and Crawford intended their hypothesis as a general mammalian principle or a human-specific one, and adjust their claims accordingly. Ketone bodies may still have been crucial for human brain evolution while being dispensable in other lineages that used different metabolic strategies.

- **The pteropodid case, where gene loss followed brain expansion (claim c1.4.3), is presented honestly but actually weakens the overarching narrative.** The inconsistency across the three lineages in the temporal relationship between HMGCS2 loss and brain expansion should be discussed more prominently, as it suggests the two phenomena may be independently driven by ecological/dietary factors rather than mechanistically linked.

**Minor Comments**

- Two species (proboscis monkey and lesser Egyptian jerboa) could not be assessed due to assembly gaps. The authors should note whether future genome assemblies for these species could alter their conclusions about the number of independent loss events.

- The characterization of HMGCS2 as "the rate-limiting enzyme" relies on a single reference (Hegardt, 1999). A brief statement of the biochemical basis for this characterization would strengthen the introduction.

- RELAX p-values of <3e-3, while significant, are confirmatory rather than independent evidence for gene loss given the already-documented inactivating mutations. The authors could note this explicitly to avoid giving the impression that this is additional independent support.

- The conservation implication for pteropodid bats (sensitivity to food scarcity due to HMGCS2 loss) is interesting but speculative. If retained, it should be clearly labeled as a hypothesis requiring empirical testing.

- The BDH1 loss in the shrew (which retains HMGCS2) is noted but insufficiently discussed. This exception to the correlated loss pattern deserves more attention.

- The dN/dS of 0.16 is reported as an average across all functional branches; reporting branch-specific values or at minimum the range would strengthen this analysis.

**Recommendation**

Major Revisions

While the genomic evidence for three independent losses of HMGCS2 is convincing (31/47 claims receive high support), three claims receive low support and several medium-support claims central to the paper's broadest conclusions — particularly regarding alternative metabolic strategies, the physiological consequences of gene loss, and the relationship to brain evolution — require either additional evidence, substantially tempered language, or both, before the paper meets eLife's standard that central claims be well-supported by the data presented.
