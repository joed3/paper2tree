**eLife Assessment**
This valuable study identifies a post-translational modification signature—Ser13 phosphorylation and caspase-6 cleavage of GFAP—associated with the most severe form of Alexander disease, using a combination of patient brain tissue and iPSC-derived astrocytes. While certain correlative findings are solid, the central mechanistic claims linking phosphorylation to caspase cleavage and aggregation rest on incomplete evidence, and the study would benefit from additional patient lines, more rigorous statistical approaches, and interventional experiments.

**Summary**
The authors investigate post-translational modifications of GFAP in Alexander disease (AxD), a fatal neurological disorder caused by dominant gain-of-function mutations in GFAP. Using phospho-proteomics on post-mortem brain tissue from 13 AxD patients carrying 10 different mutations, they identify Ser13 phosphorylation as the most abundant GFAP phospho-peptide, selectively elevated in patients who died very young (median 1.7 years). They demonstrate that phospho-mimetic S13D/S13E mutations abolish GFAP filament assembly and promote aggregation in SW13vim- cells and in vitro. Using iPSC-derived astrocytes from one AxD patient (R239C mutation) with CRISPR-corrected isogenic controls, they show that pSer13-GFAP localizes to perinuclear aggregates adjacent to nuclear invaginations. They further report a correlation between Ser13 phosphorylation and caspase-6-mediated GFAP cleavage, and demonstrate that caspase-6 expression is elevated specifically in young AxD patient brains. The authors propose that this PTM signature may serve as a biomarker for disease severity and a potential therapeutic target, while acknowledging the absence of direct causal evidence.

**Strengths**
- The unbiased phospho-proteomic discovery approach using human post-mortem tissue is well-conceived and provides clinically grounded data, rather than relying solely on overexpression systems. The identification of 12 phosphorylation sites with Ser13 as the most prominent is a meaningful advance.
- The use of CRISPR/Cas9-corrected isogenic controls is rigorous and represents best practice for iPSC disease modeling. The inclusion of "CRISPR control" clones (edited on the WT allele) as an additional control for the gene editing procedure is a thoughtful experimental design.
- The in vitro assembly experiments with purified GFAP (Figure 3C) provide clean biochemical evidence that phosphorylation status at Ser13 fundamentally alters GFAP assembly properties, with S13D forming homogeneous globular structures rather than filaments.
- The iPSC-astrocyte model recapitulates several key features of AxD (elevated GFAP, high-molecular-mass oligomers, perinuclear aggregates with DAPI-positive cores, nuclear invaginations), providing validation as a disease-relevant system.
- The parallel analysis across multiple experimental platforms (patient tissue, iPSC-astrocytes, SW13vim- cells, purified protein) strengthens the overall narrative and cross-validates key findings.
- The authors are commendably transparent about the limitations of their study, explicitly stating they cannot establish causality between phosphorylation and caspase cleavage.

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**
- **Single patient iPSC line limits generalizability.** All iPSC-astrocyte experiments derive from one AxD patient (R239C). Given the central thesis that pSer13 is mutation-independent, validating key findings (perinuclear pSer13 aggregates, caspase-6 co-localization, nuclear invaginations) in at least one additional patient line with a different GFAP mutation is essential. Without this, it is unclear whether the iPSC phenotypes are specific to R239C or generalizable to AxD.

- **Confounding of mutation identity with age/severity.** The "young" and "old" patient groups carry largely non-overlapping mutations. The paper does not adequately address whether the differences in pSer13 and caspase-6 are driven by mutation identity rather than disease severity per se. Are there known genotype-phenotype correlations for these specific mutations that could explain the age-of-death differences? The outliers in each group (Figure 2B, lanes 3 and 11) deserve more discussion—what mutations do these patients carry, and do they inform the genotype vs. severity question?

- **Inadequate control group for phospho-proteomics.** Only three non-AxD controls were used for the mass spectrometry analysis, and the authors state they "did not observe significant phosphorylation of GFAP in the control subjects." The source data should be presented more transparently. Were the controls age-matched to the young AxD patients? The Western blot in Figure 2D shows 12 non-AxD controls but these appear to be mostly older individuals; young pediatric controls are needed to rule out developmental regulation of pSer13.

- **pSer13 signal in isogenic control iPSC-astrocytes undermines specificity.** The authors acknowledge detecting pSer13-GFAP in isogenic control cells (Figure 6A), attributing it to in vitro culture conditions. This significantly weakens the claim that pSer13 is disease-specific. Quantitative comparison (e.g., Western blot densitometry of pSer13 in AxD vs. isogenic iPSC-astrocytes) is needed, along with investigation of whether the culture conditions (passage number, confluence, growth factors) modulate pSer13 levels.

- **Phospho-mimetic mutations are imperfect proxies for phosphorylation.** S13D and S13E introduce a permanent negative charge that only approximates phosphoserine. The dramatically different behavior of S13D in vitro (globular structures, no aggregation) versus in cells (large aggregates) highlights this limitation and suggests cell-specific factors contribute to the phenotype. The authors should discuss this discrepancy more thoroughly and consider using phospho-specific kinase activation or inhibition in iPSC-astrocytes as a more physiologically relevant approach.

- **Mechanistic link between phosphorylation and caspase cleavage is not established.** The correlation between pSer13 and caspase-6 cleavage could reflect independent consequences of cellular stress rather than a direct mechanistic relationship. The D225E mutagenesis experiment (Figure 8) shows that blocking cleavage does not resolve aggregation, and the caspase-6 inhibitor causes filament bundling in WT GFAP. These results actually argue against a simple linear pathway from phosphorylation → cleavage → aggregation. The authors should more explicitly address alternative models.

- **Statistical concerns.** Several comparisons involve very small sample sizes (n=3 biological replicates for some experiments) analyzed with parametric tests (t-tests, ANOVA) without demonstration of normality or variance homogeneity. The mass spectrometry quantification (Figure 2A) comparing young vs. old patients uses an unpaired t-test on what appears to be non-normally distributed data with unequal variances. Non-parametric alternatives or explicit justification for parametric approaches should be provided.

- **Quantification of immunofluorescence phenotypes.** The classification of cells as containing "filaments," "aggregates," or "both" (Figures 3B, 8D) lacks clear criteria for categorization. Were scorers blinded? How were borderline cases handled? Inter-rater reliability should be reported.

**Minor Comments**
- Figure 2B: The sample order should be explicitly labeled on the blot itself (or directly below), not only referenced to a supplementary table, to improve readability.
- The electron microscopy of iPSC-astrocytes (Figure 6B) shows filamentous bundles at nuclear invaginations but no classic electron-dense Rosenthal fibers. The distinction between the in vitro aggregates and bona fide RFs should be more clearly articulated throughout the manuscript, not only in passing.
- Figure 7E: Loading different amounts of protein to normalize GFAP monomer levels is an unconventional approach. Standard equal loading with quantification normalized to a housekeeping protein would be more rigorous and transparent.
- The kinase motif analysis (Supplementary file 4) identifying candidate kinases for S13/S16/S17 phosphorylation is interesting but entirely computational. Even preliminary validation (e.g., kinase inhibitor effects on pSer13 levels in iPSC-astrocytes) would substantially strengthen the translational implications.
- The paper references "Figure 7—figure supplement 1" for brain section immunostaining in the context of Figure 9F, which appears to be a mislabeling (should likely be "Figure 9—figure supplement 1").
- The title and abstract could more clearly convey that this is primarily a correlative/descriptive study identifying disease-associated PTM signatures, rather than a mechanistic study establishing causality.

**Recommendation**
Major Revisions
The study presents valuable correlative findings linking specific GFAP PTMs to disease severity in AxD, but the reliance on a single patient iPSC line, small control cohorts, incomplete mechanistic dissection, and several statistical concerns need to be addressed before the claims are sufficiently supported for publication at eLife's standards.
