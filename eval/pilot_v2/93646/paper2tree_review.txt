**eLife Assessment**

This is a valuable study that provides convincing evidence that CaBP1 and CaBP2 cooperatively suppress CaV1.3 channel inactivation in inner hair cells to sustain synaptic transmission required for sound encoding. The multi-level experimental approach — spanning biophysical characterization, synaptic physiology, in vivo single-unit recordings, and gene rescue — is a notable strength, though certain mechanistic claims regarding exocytosis efficiency and the extrapolation from ex vivo to in vivo conditions remain incomplete.

**Summary**

Oestreicher et al. investigate the combined roles of calcium-binding proteins CaBP1 and CaBP2 at the inner hair cell (IHC) ribbon synapse using a double-knockout (DKO) mouse model. The central thesis is that CaBP1 and CaBP2 together suppress both voltage-dependent and calcium-dependent inactivation of CaV1.3 channels, thereby sustaining calcium currents and exocytosis necessary for temporally precise, fatigue-resistant sound encoding. The authors demonstrate that the DKO phenotype far exceeds that of either single knockout, with ~6-fold enhanced CDI, near-abolished sustained exocytosis at physiological holding potentials, and severely degraded spiral ganglion neuron responses in vivo. Viral delivery of CaBP2 partially rescues calcium currents, exocytosis, and ABR responses, supporting both a causal role for CaBPs in the phenotype and the translational potential of gene therapy for DFNB93. The study marshals an impressive breadth of evidence — from patch-clamp electrophysiology with Ba²⁺/Ca²⁺ substitution, through membrane capacitance measurements at multiple holding potentials, to in vivo single-unit SGN recordings and AAV-mediated rescue — to build a multilayered argument for the cooperative function of these two proteins.

**Strengths**

- **Enhanced CaV1.3 inactivation in DKO IHCs (high support):** The separation of VDI (~2-fold increase) and CDI (~6-fold increase) using Ba²⁺/Ca²⁺ substitution following the established Tadross et al. (2010) framework provides rigorous, quantitative evidence that both inactivation modes are dramatically enhanced (p < 0.0001 for both). The finding that the DKO phenotype significantly exceeds either single knockout under comparable conditions convincingly establishes cooperative function.

- **Sustained exocytosis impairment at physiological holding potentials (high support):** The systematic evaluation of exocytosis across holding potentials (–85, –65, –55 mV) is a particularly elegant aspect of the study. The demonstration that sustained exocytosis is almost completely abolished at –55 mV — a physiologically relevant resting potential — while the readily releasable pool remains intact at –85 mV, directly links channel inactivation to synaptic failure under conditions approaching those in vivo.

- **In vivo SGN recordings (high support):** The in vivo single-unit data provide compelling functional correlates of the cellular phenotype: dramatically reduced spontaneous and evoked spike rates, increased first-spike latency (12.5 vs 4.8 ms, p < 0.001), greatly increased jitter (18.3 vs 1.5, p < 0.00001), and enhanced adaptation. The systematic manipulation of stimulus repetition rate, showing recovery of onset rates at 0.5–0.2 Hz consistent with the ~5 s slow recovery time constant measured ex vivo, elegantly bridges the cellular and systems-level findings.

- **Viral rescue (high support):** The AAV-mediated CaBP2 re-expression experiment provides a critical causal test. Recovery of Ca²⁺ current inactivation (p < 0.0001), exocytosis efficiency, and ABR wave I amplitude (p ≤ 0.0001) across multiple metrics strengthens the causal chain from CaBP loss to hearing impairment. The authors' honest reporting of incomplete rescue and discussion of multiple possible explanations is commendable.

- **Normal channel expression and synapse counts (high support):** The demonstration that peak Ca²⁺ current amplitudes, CaV1.3 immunofluorescence clustering, and ribbon synapse counts (12.9 vs 12.8) are comparable between DKO and WT at 3 weeks establishes that the phenotype reflects altered channel gating rather than reduced expression or synaptopathy, which is an essential control for the interpretation of all downstream findings.

- **Recovery from prolonged inactivation (high support):** The double-exponential recovery kinetics after 2-min sine wave conditioning in DKO, contrasting with single-exponential recovery in WT, provides direct evidence for a "deep-state" inactivation that is specifically unmasked by CaBP1/2 loss. This finding is mechanistically informative and directly relevant to sustained sound encoding.

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**

- **Speculative mechanism for reduced exocytosis efficiency (weak support):** The claim that CaBPs may directly affect exocytosis beyond calcium channel modulation — potentially through CaMKII modulation influencing otoferlin Ca²⁺ affinity — is the only weakly supported claim in the study. No experimental evidence is provided: no CaBP-CaMKII interaction studies, no CaBP-otoferlin co-immunoprecipitation, and no functional assays distinguishing exocytic from endocytic contributions to the ΔCm/QCa deficit. Given that this claim is used to support the broader thesis that CaBPs have roles "beyond calcium channel modulation," the authors should either provide experimental evidence (e.g., CaMKII inhibitor effects on DKO exocytosis) or significantly temper this discussion. At minimum, the alternative explanation that altered endocytosis could account for the apparent efficiency deficit should be more explicitly considered.

- **Extrapolation from ex vivo to in vivo channel states (moderate support):** The claim that in vivo, CaBP1/2 absence drives a large fraction of CaV1.3 channels into prolonged inactivation is a central interpretive link, but it rests entirely on ex vivo recordings at room temperature with non-physiological Ca²⁺ concentrations. CDI magnitude is known to be temperature-sensitive. No direct in vivo measurement of channel availability was performed. The authors should acknowledge this limitation more explicitly and, if possible, provide estimates of how temperature correction might affect the quantitative conclusions. Even a brief discussion of published temperature coefficients for CaV1.3 CDI would strengthen this extrapolation.

- **Partial functional overlap claim lacks molecular evidence (moderate support):** The conclusion that CaBP1 and CaBP2 "partially overlap" in function is inferred solely from the DKO-worse-than-SKO genetic pattern. No molecular-level evidence (binding competition, co-IP, structure-function analysis) is provided. The distinction between "concentration-limited compensation" and "distinct properties" is acknowledged as speculative but is presented without any experiments to discriminate between them. A CaBP1 overexpression experiment (analogous to the CaBP2 rescue) would directly test whether CaBP1 can substitute for CaBP2. The authors should at minimum discuss whether the pattern could reflect non-overlapping functions converging on the same phenotype rather than true functional redundancy.

- **DPOAE variability undermines the claim of purely synaptic origin (moderate support):** Approximately half of DKO animals showed reduced DPOAEs, and DPOAE thresholds were significantly elevated at 7–13 weeks (p < 0.002). This finding is at tension with the claim that the hearing impairment is "primarily of IHC synaptic origin." The authors should provide a more thorough analysis: are animals with reduced DPOAEs the same ones with the most severe ABR phenotypes? Is there a correlation? If CaBP1/2 are expressed in OHCs (even at low levels), this could directly affect cochlear amplification. The "different setups with different calibrations" issue for the two age groups should also be addressed.

- **Selection bias in SGN recordings (acknowledged but insufficiently addressed):** The dramatic difference in recording yield (0.3 vs 2.5 neurons/hour) means that detected DKO neurons almost certainly overrepresent the least-affected population. While this is acknowledged, its implications for the quantitative characterization of the DKO phenotype are not fully discussed. The authors should explicitly state that reported spike rates, latencies, and adaptation metrics represent upper bounds of function in the DKO, and that the true population-level phenotype is likely more severe. The classification uncertainty for ~3/19 DKO units (possible cochlear nucleus neurons) should also be addressed with additional analysis if possible.

- **Paradoxical recovery from 500-ms depolarization (moderate support):** The finding that DKO IHCs recover faster than WT from a 500-ms depolarization contradicts the overall narrative of impaired recovery and is explained by a speculative Ca²⁺ clearance hypothesis without supporting Ca²⁺ imaging data. The slowest recovery being in CaBP2-rescued DKO further complicates interpretation and could reflect overexpression artifacts. This paradox should be more carefully discussed, including the alternative interpretation that VDI (predominant in DKO) may intrinsically recover faster than CDI, independent of Ca²⁺ clearance rates.

- **CaBP2 isoform used for rescue:** The L-isoform was used for viral rescue, while CaBP2-alt may be the most abundant IHC isoform (Yang et al., 2016). This could be a significant factor in incomplete rescue and should be explicitly discussed as a limitation with consideration of whether future experiments should test the alt isoform.

**Minor Comments**

- The comparison to single KO data partly relies on previously published datasets (Picher, 2017; Yang et al., 2018) acquired under potentially different experimental conditions. While this is a practical necessity, the authors should explicitly discuss any methodological differences that could affect quantitative comparisons, particularly the different tonotopic positions used for Cabp2-KO recordings.

- Sample sizes for specific experiments (Ba²⁺/Ca²⁺ substitution, recovery protocols) are sometimes stated only in supplementary materials or figure legends. These should be included in the main text for transparency.

- The claim of "complete loss" of sustained exocytosis at –55 mV should be tempered to "near-complete" or "below detection threshold," as very small responses may fall below the sensitivity of capacitance measurements.

- The ceiling artifact for tone burst ABR thresholds (assigned 100 dB when exceeding 90 dB maximum) should be explicitly noted as leading to underestimation of true threshold elevation.

- Ribbon synapse counts and CaV1.3 clustering were assessed only at 3 weeks of age. Given the progressive phenotype, later time points would be informative. If not feasible, this should be noted as a limitation.

- The very large wave I amplitudes in CaBP2-rescued animals (potentially exceeding WT) deserve more careful discussion — could this reflect overexpression-driven hypersynchronization or a CaBP1-KO-like hyperexcitability phenotype superimposed on CaBP2 rescue?

- The contralateral eGFP expression after unilateral injection should be better characterized and its potential impact on ABR measurements discussed.

**Recommendation**

Minor Revisions

The vast majority of claims (38/53) are well supported by strong evidence, and the single weakly supported claim is appropriately peripheral to the central thesis. The core findings — enhanced CaV1.3 inactivation, impaired sustained exocytosis, degraded in vivo sound encoding, and partial gene rescue — are convincingly demonstrated through convergent multi-level evidence. The essential revisions concern important but tractable issues: tempering speculative mechanistic claims, more carefully discussing the DPOAE variability and its implications, acknowledging selection bias and extrapolation limitations more explicitly, and addressing the CaBP2 isoform choice. None of these undermine the central conclusions but are necessary to meet eLife's standards for precision of claims relative to evidence.
