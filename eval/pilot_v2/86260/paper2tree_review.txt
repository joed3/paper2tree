**eLife Assessment**

This study presents a **useful** characterization of endothelial cells derived from thrombectomy specimens of acute myocardial infarction patients, proposing a model of cell-autonomous metabolic reprogramming involving a glycolysis-to-pentose phosphate pathway shunt, enhanced glutaminolysis, and mitochondrial hyperpolarization driving excess ROS production. The evidence supporting the central claims is **incomplete**, as the metabolic model relies heavily on indirect proxies (enzyme expression levels and extracellular metabolite measurements) without direct flux measurements, uses only a single control donor line, and does not experimentally test the proposed therapeutic implications.

**Summary**

The authors isolate endothelial cells from coronary thrombectomy specimens of eight AMI patients (AMIECs) and compare their phenotypic and metabolic properties to a single commercial human coronary artery endothelial cell (HCAEC) control line. They report that AMIECs display impaired growth, migration, and tubulogenesis, accompanied by a distinctive metabolic signature: reduced glucose consumption with paradoxically high lactate production, downregulation of PFKFB3 and upregulation of PFKFB4 (interpreted as a glycolysis-to-PPP shunt), elevated G6PD expression and activity, increased glutamine consumption with elevated GLS1 expression, higher intracellular glutathione, elevated ROS, and mitochondrial membrane hyperpolarization. The authors synthesize these observations into a model wherein primary mitochondrial hyperpolarization drives excess ROS, which is countered by PPP-derived NADPH and glutaminolysis-driven glutathione synthesis, and propose that pharmacological mitochondrial uncoupling could represent a therapeutic strategy.

The study addresses a genuinely important question — the cell-autonomous metabolic basis of endothelial dysfunction in AMI — using a creative patient-derived cell model. However, the central metabolic claims rest on inferential chains that lack direct experimental validation at several critical nodes, and the experimental design has significant limitations that weaken confidence in the disease-specificity of the observed phenotypes.

**Strengths**

- **G6PD expression and enzyme activity** are measured by two independent methods (Western blotting and spectrophotometric activity assay), providing the strongest evidence in the paper for PPP activation. The internal consistency of CE-45 (near-normal G6PD correlating with near-normal ROS) adds confidence to this finding.

- **Mitochondrial membrane potential** is measured with DiIC1(5) across all eight AMIECs, with appropriate CCCP controls confirming signal specificity. The 8/8 consistency of hyperpolarization is the most uniform finding in the study and is technically well-controlled.

- **Multi-assay convergence on functional deficits**: The consistent impairment across growth, migration (with mitomycin C to control for proliferation), and tubulogenesis assays across most or all eight patient-derived lines provides reasonable evidence that AMIECs are functionally abnormal in culture. The use of eight independent patient-derived lines is a strength for biological replication.

- **Internal coherence of CE-45**: The identification of one cell line (CE-45) that behaves as a partial outlier across multiple independent measurements (near-normal PFKFB3, G6PD, ROS, glucose uptake, and amino acid profile) provides a natural internal control that strengthens the overall pattern and argues against systematic technical artifacts.

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**

- **Single control donor line (pervades all claims)**: All comparisons are made against a single commercial HCAEC line. Without multiple healthy donor controls, it is impossible to distinguish AMI-specific metabolic reprogramming from normal inter-individual variation. This limitation is particularly critical for claims about metabolic "dysfunction" and "reprogramming." The authors should obtain and characterize at minimum 3–5 independent healthy donor HCAEC lines, or formally justify why the single control is sufficient by referencing published variability data.

- **No direct metabolic flux measurements (critical gap for claims c1.2, c1.3, c1.4, c1.5)**: The central metabolic narrative — a shift from glycolysis to PPP and glutamine-dependent metabolism — is constructed entirely from enzyme expression levels and extracellular metabolite consumption/production rates. No ¹³C-glucose or ¹³C-glutamine isotope tracing was performed to validate the proposed flux redistribution. Without tracer experiments, the claim that glucose-6-phosphate is "shunted" to the PPP (c1.4), that glutamine serves as "primary carbon source" (c1.5), and especially that lactate is "glutamine-derived" (c1.3) remain speculative inferences rather than demonstrated metabolic fluxes. At minimum, ¹³C-glutamine tracing to assess carbon contribution to lactate and TCA intermediates is essential.

- **Glutamine-derived lactate hypothesis is unsupported (c1.3, c1.3.3)**: The claim that high lactate production uncoupled from glucose consumption reflects glutamine-derived lactate via malic enzyme and LDH-A is the most load-bearing weakly-supported claim. Neither malic enzyme expression/activity nor LDH-A expression/activity was measured. No isotope tracing was performed. Other potential lactate sources (medium pyruvate, other amino acids, fatty acid-derived carbons) were not considered. This claim should either be experimentally substantiated or explicitly reframed as an untested hypothesis throughout the manuscript.

- **Mitochondrial hyperpolarization without mass normalization (c1.6)**: DiIC1(5) fluorescence can be confounded by differences in mitochondrial mass. No mitochondrial mass quantification (e.g., MitoTracker Green, citrate synthase activity, mtDNA copy number) was performed. If AMIECs simply have more mitochondria, the hyperpolarization conclusion is fundamentally altered. Furthermore, no direct mitochondrial respiration measurement (e.g., Seahorse OCR) was conducted, yet "primary mitochondrial dysfunction" and "coupled mitochondrial activity" are central to the model. Seahorse respirometry and mitochondrial mass quantification are essential to support these claims.

- **ROS source attribution is unresolved (c1.6.4)**: H2DCFDA is a non-specific ROS probe. No mitochondrial-specific ROS measurement (e.g., MitoSOX) was performed to establish that elevated ROS originates from mitochondria rather than NADPH oxidases or other sources. The claim that ROS "cannot be directly attributed to environmental factors" because cells share identical culture conditions (c1.6.4) contradicts the Discussion's acknowledgment that epigenetic imprints could persist from in vivo exposure. This internal inconsistency should be resolved.

- **Pharmacological actionability is not demonstrated (c1.7)**: The root claim includes "pharmacologically actionable" dysfunction, and the paper proposes mitochondrial uncoupling as a therapeutic strategy. However, no pharmacological intervention was tested in AMIECs. This claim should be removed from the abstract and conclusions unless uncoupling experiments (e.g., with low-dose FCCP or DNP) are performed in AMIECs to demonstrate ROS reduction and functional rescue.

- **NAD+ depletion and lactate-for-NAD+ regeneration hypotheses lack evidence (c1.3.4)**: No NAD+/NADH ratio measurements were performed, yet the paper proposes that high lactate production serves to replenish NAD+ depleted by mitochondrial hyperpolarization. This mechanistic claim requires direct measurement of NAD+/NADH ratios.

- **Biological vs. technical replicates**: Throughout the manuscript, n=3 replicates are reported, but whether these represent independent biological experiments or technical replicates of the same experiment is never specified. If technical replicates, the statistical framework (Student's t-test for each AMIEC vs. HCAEC) would be inappropriate, and p-values unreliable. The authors must clarify this and, if necessary, repeat key experiments with true biological replicates.

**Minor Comments**

- The mitomycin C concentration reported for migration assays (1 mg/mL) appears unusually high; typical concentrations are 1–10 µg/mL. The authors should verify this is not a typographical error, as such a high concentration could differentially affect cell viability across lines.

- The correlation between migration and tubulogenesis (R² = 0.67, 9 data points) is used to argue that "deficient tubulogenesis is linked to defective migration" (c1.1.5), but no p-value or confidence interval is reported, and the inclusion of the HCAEC control as an anchor point may inflate the correlation. The authors should report statistical significance, show the correlation with and without the HCAEC point, and consider partial correlation controlling for growth rate.

- Exceptions to the metabolic patterns (CE-61 with high glucose uptake but low PFKFB3; glycine and alanine not elevated despite other amino acids being increased) deserve explicit discussion, as they potentially inform mechanistic interpretation.

- The speculative discussion of HIF-1α, mTOR, PPARγ, and p53 as regulators of PFKFB4 (c1.4.3, c1.4.4) is presented without any supporting experimental data. These sections should be clearly demarcated as hypothesis-generating discussion, and the argument-from-exclusion (that low glycolysis rules out HIF-1α/mTOR) should acknowledge that these pathways can differentially regulate PFKFB3 and PFKFB4.

- Figure labeling appears inconsistent in places (growth curves described as Figure 1D in text but 1C in legend). A thorough cross-check of figure references is recommended.

- The GSH/GSSG ratio would be more informative than total reduced glutathione alone for assessing actual redox status, and should be measured if feasible.

**Recommendation**

Major Revisions

The paper addresses an important biological question with a creative patient-derived cell model, but only 4 of 45 claims achieve high evidentiary support while 8 are weakly supported, with critical gaps at the most load-bearing nodes of the argument (no isotope tracing, no Seahorse respirometry, no mitochondrial mass normalization, single control donor, unsubstantiated therapeutic claims). The central metabolic model, while internally coherent, remains a plausible hypothesis rather than a demonstrated mechanism, and key experiments — particularly ¹³C-glutamine tracing, Seahorse respirometry, mitochondrial mass quantification, and inclusion of multiple healthy controls — are essential before the conclusions can meet eLife's standards for a conceptual advance supported by convincing evidence.
