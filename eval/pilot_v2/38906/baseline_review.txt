**eLife Assessment**
This valuable study provides convincing genomic evidence for the independent loss of HMGCS2, the rate-limiting ketogenesis enzyme, in three mammalian lineages with relatively large brains, though the broader evolutionary claims linking gene loss to brain size evolution rest on incomplete evidence and would benefit from more rigorous dating analyses and physiological corroboration.

**Summary**
The authors report that HMGCS2, the gene encoding the mitochondrial HMG-CoA synthase required for ketone body production, has been independently lost in cetaceans (whales and dolphins), pteropodid fruit bats, and Elephantimorpha (elephants and mastodons). Using a 70-species whole genome alignment, they identify large exon deletions, frameshift mutations, splice site disruptions, and promoter deletions in all three lineages, validated by unassembled sequencing reads and extended to extinct species (mastodon) via ancient DNA data. They further show that the non-pleiotropic ketogenesis gene BDH1 is co-inactivated in cetaceans and pteropodids, and that the paralog HMGCS1 lacks a mitochondrial targeting signal, arguing against functional compensation. The paper's central conceptual claim is twofold: (1) ketogenesis is not universally essential in mammals, revealing unexpected metabolic flexibility; and (2) because HMGCS2 loss preceded brain size expansion in cetaceans and proboscideans, ketogenesis is not a universal precondition for the evolution of large mammalian brains — challenging a prominent hypothesis from human evolutionary biology.

**Strengths**
- The core genomic finding is robust and well-validated. The authors employ multiple complementary approaches — exon deletion mapping with shared breakpoints across species, frameshift/stop codon identification, splice site disruption, promoter deletion, and verification with unassembled sequencing reads (≥30 reads supporting each mutation with no ancestral allele support). The inclusion of the heterozygous 1 bp deletion in the black flying fox is a nice demonstration of ongoing pseudogenization.
- The convergent loss in three phylogenetically distant lineages is striking and strongly supports the conclusion that ketogenesis can be dispensable rather than universally essential in mammals, which is a genuinely novel insight.
- The investigation of pathway-level consequences is thorough: the authors correctly distinguish pleiotropic genes (ACAT1, HMGCL) from non-pleiotropic ones (BDH1) and show correlated loss only in the latter, consistent with evolutionary expectations.
- The use of mastodon sequence data to push the timing of HMGCS2 loss back to >24 Mya in proboscideans strengthens the evolutionary narrative considerably.
- Analysis of HMGCS1 to rule out compensatory mitochondrial retargeting, including both bioinformatic prediction (TargetP) and RNA-seq analysis of liver transcriptomes, is a necessary and well-executed control.
- The strong purifying selection on HMGCS2 in the 59 other mammals (dN/dS = 0.16) provides important context, demonstrating that loss is lineage-specific rather than reflecting a broadly dispensable gene.

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**
- **Molecular dating of gene loss carries substantial uncertainty that is insufficiently discussed.** The dating method assumes the synonymous rate of a functional gene is 70% of the neutral rate, a simplification that could introduce systematic bias. The paper should present sensitivity analyses varying this parameter and provide formal confidence intervals for the estimated loss dates. This is critical because the central evolutionary claim — that HMGCS2 loss *preceded* brain expansion in cetaceans and Elephantimorpha — depends on these estimates.
- **The brain evolution argument, while provocative, is largely correlative and somewhat underdeveloped.** The EQ values used come from different sources with different methodologies, and the relationship between gene loss timing and brain expansion timing is presented as if precise, when both are estimated with broad uncertainty. The authors should more explicitly acknowledge the overlap of confidence intervals and the limitations of EQ as a measure of brain complexity or metabolic demand.
- **No direct physiological or metabolic evidence is provided.** The paper cites a single dolphin fasting study (Ridgway, 2013) and an old fruit bat starvation report (van der Westhuyzen, 1978) but does not present any new metabolic data. While the genomic evidence is convincing for gene loss, the claim that ketogenesis is truly absent (rather than achieved through an unknown compensatory mechanism) would be strengthened by measuring ketone body levels in accessible species (e.g., captive elephants or fruit bats) or by a more systematic review of existing metabolic literature.
- **Alternative ketogenic routes are not fully excluded.** While HMGCS1 mitochondrial retargeting is reasonably ruled out, could other enzymatic activities (e.g., amino acid catabolism through the leucine degradation pathway that shares ACAT1 and HMGCL) produce low levels of ketone bodies? The paper should discuss this possibility more explicitly, even if only to argue against it.
- **Pteropodid sampling is limited and the timing of loss in this clade is ambiguous.** With only three pteropodid genomes and complete deletion in the Egyptian fruit bat, the authors cannot resolve whether loss occurred once or independently. Additional pteropodid genomes (e.g., from non-frugivorous relatives or basal pteropodids) would strengthen the analysis. At minimum, the authors should discuss this uncertainty more prominently.
- **The ecological explanations for why ketogenesis became dispensable are plausible but untested.** Gluconeogenesis from dietary amino acids in cetaceans, constant fruit availability for pteropodids, and the musth alkalinity observation in elephants are each individually reasonable, but presenting them as if they constitute a coherent adaptive narrative overstates the evidence. The elephant explanation, in particular, is extremely speculative (blood pH during musth could reflect many physiological changes).

**Minor Comments**
- The paper would benefit from a supplementary table summarizing key physiological observations (fasting tolerance, blood ketone body measurements, dietary composition) across all three lineages and outgroup species.
- Figure 1B is information-dense; consider adding a simplified schematic summarizing the shared vs. lineage-specific mutations in a more accessible format.
- The discussion of conservation implications for pteropodid bats is interesting but brief. Given eLife's broad readership, expanding this point slightly and noting specific endangered species in this family would add practical relevance.
- The statement that HMGCS2 deficiency in humans is "probably underdiagnosed" is an interesting clinical aside but is not directly supported by evidence in this paper; consider softening or citing supporting literature.
- The methods section should explicitly state the versions of all software tools used and provide the specific RELAX test statistics (k values, p-values) in the main text rather than only in supplementary files.
- Consider discussing whether the loss of ketogenesis might impose constraints on neonatal brain development in these lineages, given the importance of ketone bodies for the developing mammalian brain mentioned in the introduction.

**Recommendation**
Minor Revisions
The core genomic finding of convergent HMGCS2 loss is novel and convincingly supported, meeting eLife's standards for a significant conceptual advance in evolutionary biology; however, the broader claims about brain evolution require more rigorous treatment of dating uncertainties and a more measured interpretive framework before publication.
