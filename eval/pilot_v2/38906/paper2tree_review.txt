**eLife Assessment**

This study presents a **valuable** contribution to our understanding of mammalian metabolic evolution, supported by **solid** genomic evidence for the independent loss of HMGCS2 in three mammalian lineages. However, several of the paper's broader claims — particularly regarding alternative brain-fueling strategies and the temporal relationship between gene loss and brain expansion — extend beyond what the current evidence compellingly demonstrates, requiring more cautious framing and, where possible, additional data.

**Summary**

Jebb and Hiller investigate the evolution of HMGCS2, the rate-limiting enzyme for ketone body biosynthesis, across 70 placental mammals. They report the unexpected finding that this gene has been independently lost in three lineages — cetaceans, Elephantimorpha (elephants and mastodons), and pteropodid fruit bats — all of which possess relatively large brains. Using molecular dating, the authors argue that HMGCS2 loss preceded brain size expansion in cetaceans and elephants, leading to the conclusion that ketogenesis is not a universal precondition for the evolution of large mammalian brains. They further show that the non-pleiotropic downstream gene BDH1 is co-lost in cetaceans and pteropodids, while pleiotropic pathway genes (ACAT1, HMGCL) remain intact, and that the paralog HMGCS1 cannot compensate due to the absence of a mitochondrial targeting signal. The paper frames these findings as evidence for unexpected flexibility in mammalian energy metabolism.

**Strengths**

- **HMGCS2 gene loss is rigorously documented across all three lineages.** The genomic evidence is exceptionally thorough: all 22 cetacean mutations were validated by ≥30 unassembled sequencing reads with no support for ancestral alleles; the Egyptian fruit bat deletion was independently confirmed with a PacBio assembly; and the elephant deletion was validated across four extant taxa and two mastodon specimens with appropriate positive controls (HMGCS1 recovery). Conserved synteny with flanking genes (REG4, PHGDH) confirms orthology.

- **Shared inactivating mutations in cetaceans provide compelling phylogenetic evidence for ancestral loss.** Shared exon 1 deletion breakpoints and a shared 2 bp frameshifting insertion in exon 2 across toothed and baleen whales convincingly establish that HMGCS2 was lost before the diversification of crown cetaceans (~36 Mya).

- **The correlated loss of BDH1 in cetaceans and pteropodids, but retention of pleiotropic genes, is an elegant evolutionary pattern.** This finding — that non-pleiotropic pathway components degrade while pleiotropic ones are maintained — is exactly what evolutionary theory predicts and provides strong corroborating evidence that the ketogenesis pathway, not just HMGCS2, has been lost in these lineages.

- **Strong purifying selection on HMGCS2 in other mammals (dN/dS = 0.16 across 59 species) effectively frames the exceptional nature of the three losses.** The contrast between strong conservation in most mammals and complete loss in three independent lineages makes a compelling case that these losses are biologically significant rather than annotation artifacts.

- **The investigation of HMGCS1 as a compensatory mechanism is methodologically responsible.** TargetP analysis, Augustus gene predictions, and liver RNA-seq from two species collectively argue against the most obvious alternative explanation for maintained ketogenesis, though limitations remain (see Weaknesses).

- **The RELAX analysis confirming relaxed selection on remaining HMGCS2 sequences (p < 3e-3) in all three lineages provides quantitative evolutionary support** consistent with pseudogenization, corroborating the mutation-based evidence.

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**

- **The root claim that these findings "reveal flexibility in mammalian energy metabolism" is overclaimed.** The paper has demonstrated that a single gene is lost; it has not characterized what alternative metabolic strategies exist. The only concrete suggestion is gluconeogenesis from amino acids in dolphins (based on a single study, Ridgway 2013). For elephants, essentially no metabolic data exists. The framing should be revised to acknowledge that this is a gene-loss observation with metabolic implications that remain to be determined, rather than an insight into metabolic flexibility per se.

- **The elephant physiological evidence is the weakest element of the paper and should be more critically presented.** The claim that blood alkalinity during musth is "consistent with lack of ketogenesis" is the only weakly-supported claim in the DAG. Blood pH during musth could be explained by numerous factors (respiratory rate changes, renal function, diet, activity levels) entirely unrelated to ketogenesis. Additionally, BDH1 retention in elephants despite HMGCS2 loss is not adequately explained — this could indicate BDH1 has unknown non-ketogenic functions, or that the elephant gene loss is more recent than estimated. The authors should explicitly discuss these ambiguities.

- **The temporal precedence claims ("HMGCS2 loss preceded brain expansion") are presented more confidently than the dating uncertainties warrant.** For elephants, the gap between gene loss (lower bound 42 Mya) and brain expansion onset (upper bound 37 Mya) is only ~5 Mya — well within the uncertainty ranges of both molecular dating and paleontological EQ estimation. The molecular dating method itself relies on untested assumptions (dN/dS = 1 for pseudogene branches, synonymous rate = 70% of neutral rate). Sensitivity analyses varying these parameters should be provided, or the language should be substantially softened from "demonstrates" to "is consistent with."

- **The claim that HMGCS1 cannot compensate is supported but incomplete.** RNA-seq data is available from only two species (minke whale, Egyptian fruit bat) at a single tissue/time point — critically, no elephant data exists. Condition-specific transcripts (e.g., during fasting) could be missed entirely. TargetP has known false-negative rates, and non-canonical mitochondrial import mechanisms cannot be excluded. The authors should explicitly acknowledge these limitations and frame the conclusion as provisional.

- **The broader conclusion that "ketogenesis is not a universal precondition for large brain evolution" rests on the assumption that HMGCS2 loss equals complete absence of ketone body production, which is not directly demonstrated in any lineage.** No metabolomic data, no tissue-specific expression analyses (beyond liver RNA-seq in two species), and no experimental evidence confirm the absence of ketone bodies in these species under physiologically relevant conditions. The Ridgway (2013) dolphin study is the closest evidence but is a single older study whose detection limits are not discussed.

- **The paper does not adequately address whether large brains in these lineages evolved under conditions where fasting-induced ketogenesis would actually be relevant.** Cetaceans on high-protein marine diets and fruit bats with constant tropical fruit availability may never have relied heavily on ketogenesis for brain fuel. If so, HMGCS2 loss may be irrelevant to brain evolution rather than demonstrating "flexibility" — a distinction the paper should discuss.

- **For pteropodids, the temporal evidence actually contradicts the "loss preceded brain expansion" pattern** (gene loss estimated at 29–18 Mya likely postdates brain expansion). Since only 2 of 3 lineages support temporal precedence, the claim's strength should be calibrated accordingly, and this asymmetry should be discussed more prominently.

**Minor Comments**

- The claim that neocortex folding "positively correlates with neuron number" is oversimplified. Elephant brains have high gyrification but relatively few cortical neurons compared to humans (Herculano-Houzel 2017). This should be nuanced or removed.

- The evidence for cognitive complexity in fruit bats (only vocal learning cited) is substantially weaker than for dolphins and elephants. The paper should acknowledge this asymmetry rather than listing all three lineages equivalently.

- Exact RELAX statistics (k parameter, test statistic values) should be reported in the main text rather than solely in supplementary files.

- The 1978 van der Westhuyzen study on Egyptian fruit bat starvation sensitivity is dated; the word "frequently" is vague regarding mortality rates. If more recent data exist, they should be cited; if not, the limitations of this evidence should be noted.

- Two species (proboscis monkey, lesser Egyptian jerboa) classified as "missing" due to assembly gaps should be noted as potential future candidates for analysis as genome assemblies improve.

- The REG4 co-deletion in elephants is noted but its functional consequences are not discussed; a brief comment on whether REG4 loss could have phenotypic effects would be informative.

**Recommendation**

Major Revisions

The genomic evidence for HMGCS2 loss is compelling and the finding itself is genuinely interesting, but several central interpretive claims — particularly regarding alternative brain-fueling strategies, temporal precedence of gene loss before brain expansion, and the completeness of ketogenesis loss — are overclaimed relative to the available evidence. Revised framing, sensitivity analyses for molecular dating, explicit discussion of the elephant BDH1 retention, and acknowledgment of the gap between documenting gene loss and demonstrating metabolic consequences are needed before the paper's conclusions can be considered fully supported at eLife's standards.
