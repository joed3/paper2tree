**eLife Assessment**
This valuable study presents solid evidence that retinoic acid–dependent retrograde BDNF-TrkB signaling mediates presynaptic homeostatic plasticity at hippocampal excitatory synapses, though several central mechanistic claims—particularly regarding direct RARα-mediated translational derepression of dendritic Bdnf mRNAs—rest on incomplete evidence that would benefit from additional experiments establishing causality and ruling out alternative interpretations.

**Summary**
The authors investigate how postsynaptic retinoic acid (RA) signaling drives presynaptic changes during homeostatic synaptic plasticity at hippocampal Schaffer collateral–CA1 synapses. Using organotypic hippocampal slices from conditional knockout mice for RARα, BDNF, and TrkB, combined with region-specific AAV-Cre injections, they systematically dissect the loci of action (pre- vs. postsynaptic) for each molecule. The study reports that (1) postsynaptic RARα is required for RA-induced increases in both mEPSC amplitude and frequency; (2) RARα directly binds dendritically localized Bdnf mRNA isoforms (exons 2 and 6) via its F domain in vitro; (3) RA induces proBDNF synthesis in hippocampal synaptoneurosomes; and (4) postsynaptic BDNF and presynaptic TrkB are required for the mEPSC frequency increase but not the amplitude increase. The authors propose a model in which RA-dependent translational derepression of dendritic Bdnf mRNAs leads to retrograde BDNF signaling through presynaptic TrkB to enhance neurotransmitter release probability. This work connects RA-mediated homeostatic plasticity with BDNF retrograde signaling, extending knowledge of how postsynaptic activity sensors communicate with presynaptic terminals.

**Strengths**
- The genetic strategy using conditional knockout mice with region-specific (CA3 vs. CA1) AAV-Cre injection to dissect pre- versus postsynaptic contributions of RARα, BDNF, and TrkB is elegant and well-controlled. The systematic and consistent results across three conditional knockout lines provide a coherent picture of the signaling pathway's spatial organization.
- The use of both acute RA application and chronic CNQX treatment to induce homeostatic plasticity strengthens the conclusions by showing convergence of pharmacological and physiological induction paradigms.
- The in vitro RNA binding assay includes appropriate negative controls (GST alone, RARα LBD lacking the F domain, RARβ LBD, and non-RARα-binding mRNAs), and the selectivity for dendritically localized Bdnf isoforms (exon 2 and 6) over the somatically restricted exon 1 isoform is consistent with the proposed model.
- The study addresses an important gap in understanding how postsynaptic activity sensors communicate with the presynaptic terminal during homeostatic plasticity—a question with broad significance given the conservation of presynaptic homeostatic mechanisms across species.
- The developmental correlation between BDNF expression levels and the emergence of presynaptic homeostatic modulation provides a plausible biological context.

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**

- **Lack of evidence that BDNF synthesis is translation-dependent and transcription-independent:** The synaptoneurosome experiment (Figure 2E) shows increased proBDNF after RA treatment, but no protein synthesis inhibitor (e.g., anisomycin, cycloheximide) or transcription inhibitor (e.g., actinomycin D) controls are included. Without these, the increase cannot be definitively attributed to de novo local translation rather than other regulatory mechanisms (protein stabilization, reduced degradation). This is critical to the central claim of RARα-mediated translational derepression.

- **No demonstration that BDNF is secreted from postsynaptic neurons:** The proposed retrograde signaling model requires that newly synthesized BDNF is released from dendrites to act on presynaptic TrkB. However, BDNF secretion is never measured. Experiments measuring BDNF release (e.g., ELISA from conditioned media, BDNF-pHluorin imaging) or showing that extracellular BDNF scavenging (TrkB-Fc) blocks the frequency increase would substantially strengthen the model.

- **The RNA binding assay is performed in vitro with purified proteins and total hippocampal RNA, which does not demonstrate that RARα binds Bdnf mRNAs in neurons:** In vivo validation (e.g., RARα RNA immunoprecipitation or CLIP from neuronal cultures/tissue) would be necessary to confirm this interaction occurs in a cellular context. Additionally, the RT-PCR readout is semi-quantitative at best—qPCR would provide more rigorous quantification of enrichment.

- **No causal link between RARα-Bdnf mRNA binding and the functional outcome:** While the binding and the functional data are individually solid, no experiment directly connects them. For instance, does expressing a Bdnf construct lacking the RARα-binding motifs fail to rescue the frequency phenotype in postsynaptic BDNF KO neurons? Without such experiments, the pathway from RARα binding → BDNF translation → retrograde signaling remains correlative.

- **Absence of rescue experiments:** Can exogenous BDNF or a TrkB agonist rescue the mEPSC frequency deficit in postsynaptic BDNF KO or presynaptic TrkB KO conditions? Such experiments would provide strong evidence for the epistatic relationship proposed.

- **Verification of conditional knockout efficiency is not shown:** While infection rates are visually confirmed (Figure 1—figure supplement 1C), protein-level verification of BDNF or TrkB deletion in the targeted regions is not provided. Given that BDNF protein has a relatively long half-life, confirmation of effective protein depletion within the experimental timeline (21–25 DIV) is important.

- **Statistical methodology concern:** The combination of two-way ANOVA followed by Mann-Whitney tests is unconventional. Parametric ANOVA assumptions and nonparametric post-hoc tests are methodologically inconsistent. The authors should either use nonparametric approaches throughout or justify the mixed approach. Additionally, for Figure 3C and 3F, the ANOVA interaction terms for frequency are not significant (p > 0.05), yet pairwise comparisons are still presented, which inflates Type I error risk.

- **Interpretation of mEPSC frequency changes:** While the authors provide PPR data and silent synapse analysis (Figure 1—figure supplement 1), these are only performed in WT + RA conditions. PPR and/or coefficient of variation analyses in the conditional knockout conditions would strengthen the claim that presynaptic release probability is the mechanism underlying frequency changes. Additionally, the failure rate analysis uses a relatively small sample size and the power to detect silent synapses may be limited.

**Minor Comments**
- The RA concentration differs between synaptoneurosome experiments (1 µM) and slice experiments (10 µM). The rationale for this difference should be explained, and whether 1 µM RA in slices produces similar effects should be discussed.
- The paper would benefit from a schematic of the experimental design showing the AAV injection strategy and timeline more clearly in the main figures rather than only in the supplement.
- The discussion extensively reviews BDNF literature but could be more focused on the specific implications and limitations of the present findings.
- Figure 2D shows semi-quantitative RT-PCR gel images without quantification. Even if semi-quantitative, densitometric analysis across replicates would be informative.
- The manuscript mentions that mIPSC frequency is also reduced by RA in older neurons (Figure 1—figure supplement 1D), which is an interesting finding that is largely unexplored. The authors should discuss whether BDNF-TrkB signaling might also be involved at inhibitory synapses or whether a different retrograde mechanism operates there.
- The synaptoneurosome preparation quality control (Figure 2—figure supplement 1D) shows PSD95 enrichment but does not include a presynaptic marker, which would help assess whether the preparation truly isolates synaptic compartments or also contains significant presynaptic material.
- n/N notation is used inconsistently across figure legends. Clarifying what constitutes an independent experiment (different pups vs. different slices from the same pup) would aid interpretation.

**Recommendation**
Major Revisions
The study addresses an important question and employs an elegant genetic strategy, but the central mechanistic claim—that RARα-mediated translational derepression of dendritic Bdnf mRNAs drives retrograde presynaptic homeostatic plasticity—requires additional experimental support to establish causality and rule out alternative mechanisms, which is necessary to meet eLife's standard for convincing evidence supporting a significant conceptual advance.
