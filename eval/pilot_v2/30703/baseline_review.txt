**eLife Assessment**
This valuable study presents a novel pathway linking wound-induced ROS signaling to vimentin expression and collagen-guided epithelial projections during zebrafish caudal fin regeneration. While the live imaging data are compelling and reveal a previously unappreciated mechanism, several central mechanistic claims rest on incomplete evidence due to reliance on pharmacological inhibitors with known off-target effects and morpholino-based loss-of-function approaches without stable genetic mutant confirmation.

**Summary**
The authors investigate how early wound-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) regulate tissue regeneration in larval zebrafish caudal fins. They generate a vimentin promoter reporter line and demonstrate that wounding induces vimentin-positive (vim+) cells at the wound margin in a ROS- and NFκB-dependent manner. Using morpholino knockdown and CRISPR F0 mosaics, they show vimentin is required for fin regeneration. Through elegant long-duration multiphoton and confocal live imaging, they discover that collagen fibers (visualized by SHG) form projections at the wound edge that guide epithelial cell extensions, and that this process depends on vimentin and collagen integrity. They propose a model in which ROS → NFκB → vimentin → collagen expression/reorganization → epithelial projections → regenerative outgrowth.

**Strengths**
- The long-duration live imaging combining SHG microscopy with fluorescence reporters is technically impressive and provides compelling visual evidence for collagen fibers guiding epithelial projections during wound healing — a novel and conceptually interesting finding.
- The vimentin reporter line is well-characterized, with expression patterns matching published in situ data and appropriate induction during oncogenic Ras-driven EMT, making it a useful tool for the community.
- The burn wound model is a creative complementary approach that independently supports the role of collagen fiber integrity in projection formation and healing.
- The integration of multiple imaging modalities (confocal, SHG, LC-PolScope, bright field) provides rich, multi-scale characterization of the wound healing process.
- The study connects early signaling events (ROS) to structural/mechanical outcomes (collagen-guided epithelial projections), bridging a conceptual gap in the regeneration field.

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**
- **Vimentin loss-of-function is insufficiently validated.** The primary loss-of-function relies on morpholinos, which are known to have off-target effects. The CRISPR approach uses only F0 mosaics, which have variable and unquantified mutation efficiency. Stable vimentin mutant lines (F2 or later) with confirmed biallelic loss are needed to firmly establish that vimentin is required for regeneration. The RNA rescue experiment partially addresses this, but stable mutants would be far more convincing for a study of this scope.

- **Pharmacological inhibitor specificity is a major concern.** DPI is a broad flavoprotein inhibitor, not specific to NADPH oxidases. Withaferin A (WA) is acknowledged to directly bind and disrupt vimentin intermediate filaments, yet is used to implicate NFκB in vimentin transcriptional regulation — this circular logic undermines the NFκB pathway conclusion. The authors should use more specific NFκB inhibitors or genetic approaches (e.g., dominant-negative IκB) to establish the ROS → NFκB → vimentin axis.

- **The identity of vim+ wound-edge cells remains undefined.** The authors exclude epithelial cells, macrophages, and leukocytes but do not positively identify these cells. Are they fibroblasts, progenitors, or another mesenchymal population? Co-staining with additional markers (e.g., fibroblast markers, proliferation markers, or blastema markers) would significantly strengthen the manuscript.

- **The causal relationship between vimentin and collagen fiber projections is correlative.** Vimentin depletion reduces both collagen expression and fiber projections, but it is unclear whether the projection defect is due to reduced collagen production, impaired collagen organization, or both. Experiments that rescue collagen expression independently of vimentin (e.g., exogenous collagen or col1a1 overexpression) would help distinguish these possibilities.

- **Quantification of vim+ cells and projections needs improvement.** The proportion of larvae with "visible projections" is a binary, subjective measure. More quantitative metrics (projection number, length, dynamics) would strengthen the conclusions. Similarly, the EGFP intensity quantification for vim+ cells at the wound uses a simple ratio that may not adequately capture differences, and sample sizes per replicate are small (2–6 fins per treatment per replicate).

- **The BAPN experiment is weakened by toxicity.** The authors acknowledge that BAPN causes lethality between 4–5 dpf and that the contour length measurement was confounded by fin shape changes. This makes it difficult to draw firm conclusions about lysyl oxidase function in this context.

- **Statistical approaches for some experiments are underpowered.** Several qRT-PCR experiments use only 3–6 biological replicates with borderline p-values (e.g., col1a1 at 8 hpw, p=0.0779). The projection scoring experiments use n=3 replicate percentages for statistical tests, which is a very small sample for parametric testing.

- **The connection between vim+ cells and collagen organization is mechanistically unclear.** How do non-motile vim+ cells at the wound edge regulate collagen fiber extension and reorganization? Do they secrete collagen? Do they physically interact with fibers to orient them? The SHG/vim+ cell co-imaging (Figure 2K-N) shows association but not mechanism.

**Minor Comments**
- The manuscript would benefit from a clearer schematic summarizing the proposed pathway and the evidence supporting each link.
- Figure 2J: the y-axis label and explanation of the ratio metric could be clearer; confidence intervals are narrow and close to 1, raising questions about biological significance.
- The claim that vim+ cells "did not appear to arise from epithelial cells" based on live imaging (Figure 2—figure supplement 1B,C) is stated cautiously but should be further qualified given the temporal resolution limitations acknowledged by the authors.
- The paper switches between 2 dpf and 3 dpf wounding timepoints without clearly justifying this variation or discussing whether developmental stage affects outcomes.
- Some supplementary videos are referenced but difficult to evaluate in review — key frames from these videos should be more thoroughly represented in the main figures.
- The Discussion speculates about clinical relevance to human wound healing; this should be tempered given the significant differences between zebrafish larval fin regeneration and mammalian cutaneous wound repair.

**Recommendation**
Major Revisions
The core finding that collagen fibers guide epithelial projections during regeneration is novel and supported by compelling imaging, but the mechanistic pathway (ROS → NFκB → vimentin → collagen → projections) relies too heavily on pharmacological inhibitors with known off-target effects and morpholino knockdowns without stable genetic confirmation, falling short of eLife's standard for convincing mechanistic evidence.
