**eLife Assessment**
This valuable study presents a creative approach to dissociating feedforward and recurrent visual processing using ultra-rapid serial visual presentation combined with MEG and MEG-fMRI fusion, but the central claim that the observed dynamics reflect feedback processing from high-level to early visual cortex rests on incomplete evidence, as several alternative explanations are not adequately ruled out.

**Summary**
The paper uses ultra-rapid serial visual presentation (ultra-RSVP) at 17 ms and 34 ms per image, combined with MEG recordings and MEG-fMRI fusion, to dissociate feedforward from recurrent processing in the human ventral visual pathway. The authors report two temporally distinct processing stages: an initial bottom-up sweep peaking progressively earlier with faster presentation rates (~96–100 ms), and a subsequent emergence of categorical (face vs. object) information peaking progressively later (~169–197 ms). They argue this inverse relationship between the two peaks indexes recurrent processing demands, and use MEG-fMRI fusion to show bimodal activity in early visual cortex (EVC), with a second EVC peak temporally coinciding with categorical representations in inferior temporal cortex (IT), interpreted as feedback from IT to EVC. The study aims to resolve a longstanding debate about the relative contributions of feedforward and recurrent processing to object recognition, using an ecologically motivated paradigm with natural images.

**Strengths**
- The ultra-RSVP paradigm is a creative and well-motivated approach to temporally segregating processing stages that normally overlap, and the behavioral data confirm that participants can still perform face detection under these extreme conditions.
- The combination of time-resolved MEG decoding with MEG-fMRI fusion provides a principled multimodal framework to characterize both temporal dynamics and spatial locus of visual representations.
- The temporal generalization analysis provides clear evidence that RSVP suppresses sustained neural activity, yielding transient dynamics that facilitate the separation of processing stages.
- The inverse relationship between early peak latency and categorical peak latency across conditions is a striking and well-documented finding that constrains models of ventral stream processing.
- Multiple converging analyses (grand total decoding, within-category decoding, categorical division, MEG-fMRI fusion) provide internally consistent results, strengthening the overall narrative.
- The discussion thoughtfully addresses implications for computational models incorporating recurrence.

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**
- **fMRI-MEG fusion confound**: The fMRI data were collected from a separate cohort under a 500 ms per picture viewing condition, not under RSVP conditions. The spatial representations captured by fMRI during slow viewing may differ substantially from those during ultra-RSVP (e.g., due to differential mask interference, incomplete object representations, or different attentional states). This means the "IT" and "EVC" spatial templates used in the fusion are based on normal viewing representations and may not accurately reflect the actual spatial locus of processing during RSVP. The authors should explicitly acknowledge this limitation and, ideally, discuss or model how RSVP-specific fMRI patterns might alter the fusion results.

- **Alternative explanations for the second EVC peak**: The second peak in EVC could reflect processing of mask stimuli presented after the target rather than feedback from IT. Since masks are objects presented at the same retinal location, late EVC activity is expected from ongoing mask processing. The authors need to provide evidence or analyses to distinguish target-related feedback from mask-driven feedforward activity in EVC (e.g., by examining whether the second EVC peak carries target-specific information rather than mask information).

- **Signal-to-noise confound for onset latencies**: The authors interpret later onset latencies with faster presentation rates as evidence that "recurrent activity is already incorporated when the first decoding onsets emerge." However, reduced stimulus duration yields weaker neural signals, and classifiers require stronger signal-to-noise ratios to exceed chance—naturally pushing onset latencies later without invoking recurrent processing. This alternative must be explicitly addressed.

- **Categorical information delays may not require recurrence**: The delayed emergence of categorical information under RSVP could reflect degraded feedforward signals requiring more evidence accumulation time, rather than increased recurrent processing per se. A purely feedforward system with noisy inputs could also show delayed category boundaries. The authors should discuss why a feedforward account with degraded input is insufficient and what specific predictions distinguish recurrence from slow/noisy feedforward accumulation.

- **Different participant cohorts**: Three different cohorts were used for the RSVP MEG, 500 ms MEG, and fMRI experiments. While between-subject comparisons are used, individual differences in anatomy, attention, and visual processing could introduce systematic biases, particularly for the MEG-fMRI fusion which pairs group-averaged MEG RDMs with individual fMRI RDMs from a completely different sample. The impact of this design choice on the reliability of the fusion results should be assessed or discussed.

- **Non-significant second EVC peak at 17 ms/picture**: The authors acknowledge that the second EVC peak in the 17 ms condition does not reach significance, yet they interpret it as showing "comparable temporal dynamics" to the 34 ms condition and mark it on the figure. This is problematic—non-significant results should not be treated as confirmatory. The interpretation should be tempered accordingly.

- **Low-level feature confounds**: Face and object images likely differ in low-level visual properties (spatial frequency content, contrast energy, aspect ratio). The categorical division results could partly reflect these differences. The authors should include analyses controlling for low-level features (e.g., using image similarity metrics as covariates or showing that within-category decoding patterns cannot explain the categorical effect).

**Minor Comments**
- The paper would benefit from showing the actual stimulus set (or representative examples under permissive licensing) in the figures, as the placeholder images make it harder to evaluate stimulus properties.
- Table 1 and Table 2 summarizing latencies are helpful but should include statistical test results (p-values) for all pairwise comparisons, not only significance flags.
- The claim that motor preparation signals are unlikely before 200 ms cites Thorpe et al. (1996), but more recent work suggests preparatory signals can emerge earlier under high-certainty conditions. The delayed response design mitigates this, but the fixed button mapping for face-present/absent could still introduce expectation-related signals.
- The MDS visualizations (Figure 3c) are informative but would benefit from quantification (e.g., silhouette scores or cluster separation indices) rather than qualitative assessment.
- The choice of a 20 Hz low-pass filter is aggressive and could attenuate high-frequency components relevant to rapid processing. The authors should justify this choice or show results are robust to higher cutoff frequencies.
- The manuscript occasionally conflates "recurrent processing" (which includes local lateral connections) with "feedback processing" (top-down from higher areas). Greater terminological precision would improve clarity.

**Recommendation**
Major Revisions
The paradigm is creative and the multimodal approach is commendable, but the central mechanistic claim—that the observed dynamics specifically reflect feedback from IT to EVC—requires stronger evidence to rule out alternative explanations, particularly mask-driven activity in EVC, signal-to-noise confounds on onset latencies, and the validity of using slow-viewing fMRI templates for RSVP fusion analyses.
