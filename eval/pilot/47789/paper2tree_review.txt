**eLife Assessment**

This valuable study provides solid evidence that site-specific phosphorylation of GFAP at Ser13 and caspase-6-mediated cleavage are associated with severe, early-onset Alexander disease, though the mechanistic claims linking these PTMs to disease pathogenesis rest on incomplete evidence due to small sample sizes, unresolved contradictions between in vitro and cellular systems, and reliance on a single patient mutation in the iPSC model.

**Summary**

Battaglia et al. investigate post-translational modifications (PTMs) of GFAP in Alexander disease (AxD), a fatal neurodegenerative disorder caused by dominant GFAP mutations. Using phospho-proteomics on post-mortem brain tissue from 13 AxD patients harboring 10 different mutations, the authors identify Ser13 as the most abundantly phosphorylated residue, with pSer13-GFAP selectively elevated in patients who died young (median 1.7 years) compared to those who survived longer (median 38 years). They demonstrate that phospho-mimic mutations (S13D, S13E) promote GFAP aggregation in SW13vim⁻ cells and abolish filament assembly in vitro, and that pSer13-GFAP marks the core of perinuclear aggregates adjacent to nuclear invaginations in iPSC-derived AxD astrocytes. The authors further show that caspase-6 expression and caspase-6-cleaved GFAP are selectively elevated in young AxD patients, correlating with elevated pSer13-GFAP, and propose this "PTM signature" as a marker for the most severe form of AxD.

The study employs a commendable multi-system approach—mass spectrometry, phospho-specific antibodies, purified protein assembly, SW13vim⁻ transfection, and CRISPR-corrected iPSC-astrocytes—to build converging evidence for the central claims. The authors are appropriately cautious about causality, explicitly stating they cannot determine whether these PTMs drive or result from disease severity. However, several critical limitations related to sample size, model system generalizability, and internal data contradictions temper the strength of the conclusions.

**Strengths**

- The identification of pSer13-GFAP as a severity-associated modification is supported by two independent methods (mass spectrometry and KT13 phospho-specific antibody western blot) across 13 AxD patients representing 10 different GFAP mutations, providing reasonable evidence that this PTM is independent of mutation position (primary claim c1.1).

- The iPSC-astrocyte model is rigorously designed with CRISPR/Cas9-generated isogenic controls, comprehensive quality control (karyotyping, TaqMan Scorecard, off-target sequencing), and recapitulates multiple AxD features including elevated GFAP, high-molecular-mass oligomers, perinuclear aggregates with RF-like staining characteristics (GFAP-positive periphery, DAPI-positive core), and nuclear invaginations (claim c1.6, high support).

- The demonstration that caspase-6 protein is expressed in brain tissue from all 8 young AxD patients but is undetectable in older patients (claim c1.5), with age-matched non-AxD controls ruling out a developmental/age effect (p<0.01, two-way ANOVA), represents a clear and novel finding with potential implications for understanding disease pathogenesis.

- The pharmacological (Ac-VEID-CHO inhibitor) and genetic (D225E mutagenesis) approaches to characterize caspase-6-mediated GFAP cleavage provide complementary evidence, with the D225E mutation reducing cleavage by >90% (p<0.0001) and the inhibitor significantly reducing cleaved S13D-GFAP (claims c1.4.1.2 and c1.4.2.1).

- The authors demonstrate intellectual honesty throughout, explicitly acknowledging that: (i) they cannot establish causality between phosphorylation and cleavage (claim c1.4.3); (ii) neither caspase-6 inhibition nor cleavage site mutagenesis fully resolves aggregation (claim c1.4.2.3); and (iii) perinuclear aggregates in iPSC-astrocytes are not identical to Rosenthal fibers (claim c1.3.3.2).

- The convergence of pSer13-GFAP, caspase-6 expression, and cleaved GFAP across patient brain tissue, SW13vim⁻ cells, and iPSC-astrocytes strengthens the overall narrative despite individual system limitations.

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**

- **Unresolved internal contradiction regarding aggregation mechanism (claim c1.2.4):** Purified S13D-GFAP forms homogeneous, non-aggregating globular structures in vitro, yet S13D forms large aggregates in cells. This directly undermines the claim that "Ser13 phosphorylation promotes GFAP aggregation" (claim c1.2). The authors note this discrepancy but do not adequately discuss or experimentally address what cellular factors convert non-aggregating globules into aggregates. This is a central interpretive gap: does pSer13 promote aggregation, or does it prevent normal assembly while cellular factors (including potentially caspase-6 cleavage) drive aggregation? The authors must provide a more thorough discussion of this tension and ideally test whether the addition of GFAP cleavage fragments to S13D in vitro promotes aggregation.

- **Small sample sizes limit statistical robustness (claims c1.1, c1.1.1, c1.1.2):** The core finding of pSer13-GFAP elevation in young AxD patients rests on 8 young vs. 5 old patients with acknowledged outliers in each group (Figure 2B, lanes 3 and 11). Only 3 non-AxD controls were used for mass spectrometry. With p-values at the 0.05 threshold and small group sizes, the statistical power is marginal. The authors should present effect sizes and confidence intervals, discuss the outliers more thoroughly (particularly why lane 3—a young patient—shows low pSer13, and lane 11—an older patient—shows elevated signal), and consider non-parametric tests given the small sample sizes and potential non-normality.

- **Single mutation in iPSC model limits generalizability (claims c1.3, c1.6):** Only the R239C mutation was modeled in iPSC-astrocytes, yet AxD encompasses >70 different GFAP mutations. The central thesis—that diverse mutations converge on a common PTM signature—would be substantially strengthened by demonstrating pSer13-GFAP aggregation and nuclear invaginations in iPSC-astrocytes from at least one additional patient with a different mutation (e.g., R79H, the most common AxD mutation).

- **Confounds between disease severity and age at death (root claim):** The young vs. old patient comparison conflates disease severity with age-dependent biological differences (developmental stage, immune maturation, post-mortem tissue quality). While age-matched non-AxD controls partially address this, the authors should more explicitly discuss whether pSer13-GFAP and caspase-6 could reflect the general response of immature brain tissue to proteotoxic stress rather than being specific to severe AxD. Post-mortem intervals for each sample should be reported and their potential impact on phosphorylation state discussed.

- **Caspase-6 inhibitor specificity (claims c1.4.1.2, c1.4.2.2):** Ac-VEID-CHO at 10 µM can inhibit caspase-3, caspase-8, and other caspases. No dose-response curve was performed, and no genetic approach (e.g., caspase-6 siRNA/shRNA) was used to confirm specificity in the cleavage and aggregation experiments. The authors should either perform caspase-6 knockdown experiments or present dose-response data demonstrating that the observed effects occur at concentrations selective for caspase-6.

- **pSer13-GFAP detected in isogenic control iPSC-astrocytes (claim c1.3.1.1):** The observation of pSer13-GFAP in control cells, attributed speculatively to "in vitro culture conditions," significantly weakens the claim that pSer13 is a disease-specific marker. The authors should quantify pSer13 signal intensity in AxD vs. control iPSC-astrocytes (e.g., by western blot of iPSC-astrocyte lysates probed with KT13) and discuss whether the in vivo brain tissue data (Figure 2D) showing absent pSer13 in controls is more representative.

- **Translational claims lack experimental support (claims c1.7, c1.7.1, c1.7.2):** The suggestions that pSer13-GFAP could serve as a biomarker in CSF/blood or that kinase inhibition could provide therapeutic strategies are entirely speculative with no supporting data. While the authors are appropriately cautious in their language, these claims should be more clearly delineated as future directions rather than implications of the current work.

- **Permissive FDR threshold for phospho-proteomics (claim c1.1.1.1):** A 5% FDR cutoff is relatively permissive for phospho-proteomic analysis. The authors should report how many of the 12 phosphorylation sites pass a more stringent 1% FDR threshold, or justify the 5% cutoff.

**Minor Comments**

- The criteria for categorizing cells as having "filaments," "aggregates," or "both" in the SW13vim⁻ quantification (Figure 3B) should be explicitly defined, ideally with example images for each category.

- Filament length in S13A-expressing cells is described as "shorter" compared to WT but this appears qualitative. Quantitative filament length measurements from EM images would strengthen this claim.

- The form of caspase-6 detected in patient brain tissue (pro-enzyme vs. active cleaved form) should be specified, as this distinction is critical for interpreting whether caspase-6 is merely expressed or actively functioning in young AxD brains.

- The two-way ANOVA results for caspase-6 expression (Figure 9C) should report the interaction term and main effects, not just the post-hoc comparison.

- The nature of the DAPI-positive material within aggregate cores in iPSC-astrocytes is not characterized. While the authors draw parallels to in vivo RF DAPI staining, it would be informative to test whether this represents trapped nucleic acids (e.g., with RNase/DNase treatment).

- Figure 7E: the different protein loading amounts between AxD and isogenic control lanes to "normalize GFAP monomer levels" makes interpretation of the cleaved fragment comparison difficult. A standard loading with quantification of both full-length and cleaved GFAP, normalized to a housekeeping protein, would be more straightforward.

- The discussion of nuclear invaginations and their potential functional consequences is interesting but remains correlative. The authors could strengthen this section by quantifying invagination frequency and depth across AxD and control iPSC-astrocytes.

**Recommendation**

Major Revisions

While the study presents a largely well-supported narrative (36/53 claims with high support) using multiple complementary approaches, the unresolved contradiction between in vitro and cellular aggregation phenotypes of S13D-GFAP, the reliance on a single patient mutation in the iPSC model, marginal statistical power from small patient cohorts with acknowledged outliers, and the lack of genetic confirmation of caspase-6 specificity represent essential gaps that must be addressed before the central claims meet eLife's standards for a convincing mechanistic advance.
