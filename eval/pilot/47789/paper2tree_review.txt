**eLife Assessment**

This study presents a **valuable** investigation into post-translational modifications of GFAP in Alexander disease, identifying phosphorylation at Ser13 and caspase-6-mediated cleavage as correlates of disease severity. The evidence is **solid** overall, supported by convergent data from mass spectrometry, patient tissue, and iPSC models, though several central mechanistic claims rest on incomplete evidence, particularly regarding the causal relationship between phosphorylation and aggregation and the internal contradiction between in vitro and cellular assembly data.

**Summary**

This paper investigates the hypothesis that disease-causing GFAP mutations in Alexander disease (AxD) perturb post-translational modifications, creating a "PTM signature" associated with the most severe disease forms. Using phospho-proteomics on post-mortem brain tissue from 13 AxD patients carrying 10 different mutations, the authors identify Ser13 as the most abundantly phosphorylated residue and show that pSer13-GFAP is selectively elevated in patients who died young. They extend these findings using iPSC-derived astrocytes carrying the R239C mutation with isogenic CRISPR-corrected controls, demonstrating that pSer13-GFAP localizes to perinuclear aggregates associated with nuclear invaginations. Phospho-mimic mutations (S13D, S13E) promote aggregation in cells and are associated with increased caspase-6-mediated cleavage of GFAP. The authors further show that caspase-6 protein is selectively expressed in young AxD patient brain tissue and correlates with cleaved GFAP, leading them to propose pSer13 phosphorylation and caspase-6 cleavage as novel markers of severe AxD.

**Strengths**

- **Convergent multi-method identification of pSer13-GFAP:** The finding that Ser13 is the most abundantly phosphorylated GFAP residue in AxD, validated by both mass spectrometry and the phospho-specific KT13 antibody across 13 patients with 10 different mutations, is well-supported and represents a genuinely novel observation in the AxD field.

- **Rigorous iPSC model with isogenic controls:** The iPSC-astrocyte model recapitulating elevated GFAP, perinuclear aggregates with RF-like characteristics (GFAP-positive periphery, DAPI-positive center), high-molecular-mass oligomers, and nuclear invaginations is compelling. The CRISPR-corrected isogenic controls and the creative "CRISPR control" (editing the WT allele while retaining the mutation) provide rigorous genetic controls with comprehensive quality control (karyotyping, pluripotency markers, off-target analysis).

- **Caspase-6 expression pattern in patient tissue:** The binary detection of caspase-6 protein in all 8 young AxD patients versus undetectable levels in older patients, combined with age-matched non-AxD controls ruling out a developmental age effect (two-way ANOVA, p<0.01), is a striking and clearly presented finding.

- **Pharmacological and genetic validation of caspase-6 cleavage:** The caspase-6 inhibitor experiment reducing S13D-GFAP cleavage, combined with the D225E cleavage site mutant reducing cleavage by >90% (p<0.0001), provides strong biochemical evidence that caspase-6 acts at D225 on GFAP. The augmented cleavage when S13D is combined with R79H demonstrates disease-relevant synergistic effects.

- **Honest reporting of complications:** The authors' acknowledgment that in vitro aggregates are not identical to Rosenthal fibers, that pSer13 is detected in isogenic control cells, that causality between phosphorylation and cleavage is not established, and that neither caspase-6 inhibition nor cleavage site mutagenesis fully resolves aggregation reflects scientific integrity and strengthens the paper's credibility.

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**

- **Internal contradiction between in vitro and cellular S13D phenotypes requires resolution:** Purified S13D-GFAP forms homogeneous, non-aggregation-prone globular structures in vitro, yet forms large aggregates in cells. This directly undermines the central claim that "Ser13 phosphorylation promotes GFAP aggregation." The authors must explicitly address this contradiction and reframe the claim — the data more accurately support that Ser13 phosphorylation *prevents filament assembly*, with aggregation being a secondary cellular response to unassembled protein. This distinction has significant implications for the proposed pathomechanism and should be discussed in depth, with experiments (or at minimum, clear hypotheses) addressing what cellular factors convert assembly-incompetent globules into aggregates.

- **Small sample sizes and statistical concerns weaken the patient tissue claims:** The dichotomous separation of "young" (n=8) versus "old" (n=5) AxD patients, with acknowledged outliers in both groups, provides marginal statistical evidence (p<0.05 by unpaired t-test without multiple testing correction across 12 phospho-sites). The 14-year gap between groups (youngest "old" patient: 27 years; oldest "young" patient: 14 years) makes it unclear whether this represents a genuine bimodal distribution or a sampling artifact. The authors should present the data as a continuous correlation with age at death, apply appropriate multiple testing corrections, and discuss the outliers in each group rather than relying on an arbitrary dichotomy.

- **Generalizability limited by single iPSC mutation:** All iPSC experiments use only R239C-GFAP, one of >70 known AxD mutations. Since the central thesis is that the PTM signature operates "independently of the position of the disease mutation," at least one additional mutation (ideally from a different GFAP domain) should be examined in the iPSC system. Without this, the generalizability of the perinuclear aggregation, nuclear invagination, and caspase-6 co-localization findings remains uncertain.

- **Phospho-mimic limitations are insufficiently addressed:** The reliance on S13D/S13E as surrogates for phosphorylation is a significant caveat, particularly given that S13A also shows abnormal assembly (shorter filaments), suggesting that *any* substitution at this position disrupts GFAP assembly. No experiment demonstrates that actual phosphorylation (e.g., by an identified kinase) promotes aggregation. The authors should either perform kinase-mediated phosphorylation experiments or substantially temper claims about phosphorylation "promoting" aggregation.

- **The "PTM signature" concept implies mechanistic linkage that is not demonstrated:** The co-occurrence of pSer13 phosphorylation and caspase-6 cleavage in severe AxD could reflect two independent consequences of cellular stress rather than a coordinated signature. No experiment directly tests whether phosphorylation at Ser13 changes the biochemical susceptibility of GFAP to caspase-6 cleavage (e.g., an in vitro cleavage assay comparing phosphorylated vs. unphosphorylated GFAP as substrate). Without such evidence, the authors should present these as correlated but potentially independent markers rather than a unified "signature."

- **Caspase-6 specificity as an AxD marker is not established:** The claim of caspase-6 as a "new marker" for severe AxD lacks disease-specificity controls — no non-AxD neurodegenerative disease tissues (e.g., other leukodystrophies, pediatric neurodegeneration) were examined. Given that caspase-6 upregulation is documented in Huntington's and Alzheimer's diseases, the observed elevation may reflect a general severe neurodegeneration response. The authors should either include additional disease controls or qualify this claim substantially.

- **Therapeutic and diagnostic claims lack supporting evidence:** The suggestions regarding CSF/blood biomarker utility and kinase inhibition therapy (two of three low-support claims) are entirely speculative. The finding that neither caspase-6 inhibition nor cleavage site mutagenesis resolves aggregation actually argues against simple PTM-targeted therapeutic approaches. These claims should be clearly labeled as speculative hypotheses or removed.

**Minor Comments**

- The criteria for categorizing cells as containing "filaments," "aggregates," or "both" in the SW13vim- system (Figure 3B) should be explicitly defined, and blinding of the scoring should be documented.

- The form of caspase-6 detected by immunoblot (pro-enzyme vs. active cleaved form) should be specified, as this has implications for interpreting the functional significance of caspase-6 detection.

- The presence of pSer13-GFAP in isogenic control iPSC-astrocytes, attributed to "in vitro culture conditions," deserves more careful discussion — this observation has important implications for whether pSer13 is truly disease-specific or becomes pathological only in the context of a GFAP mutation.

- Quantification of nuclear invagination frequency, depth, and relationship to aggregate size would strengthen the descriptive association between perinuclear aggregates and nuclear deformation.

- The Ac-VEID-CHO inhibitor experiment would be strengthened by a dose-response curve and ideally a genetic approach (caspase-6 knockdown) to confirm target specificity.

- PSM counts as a measure of phospho-peptide abundance should be discussed with appropriate caveats regarding ionization bias; label-free quantification by extracted ion chromatogram peak areas would be more rigorous.

**Recommendation**

Major Revisions

The paper addresses an important question in AxD biology and presents several well-supported observations (36/53 claims at high support), but the central mechanistic claims — particularly that phosphorylation "promotes aggregation" despite contradictory in vitro data, and that pSer13 and caspase-6 cleavage constitute a linked "PTM signature" — require additional experimental support or substantial reframing. The limited generalizability (single iPSC mutation), small patient cohorts with marginal statistics, and unresolved internal contradictions must be addressed before the paper meets eLife's standard for claims that advance conceptual understanding of disease mechanisms.
