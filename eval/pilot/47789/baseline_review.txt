**eLife Assessment**
This valuable study identifies a post-translational modification signature — Ser13 phosphorylation and caspase-6-mediated cleavage of GFAP — that correlates with the most severe form of Alexander disease, but the mechanistic claims linking phosphorylation to aggregation and caspase cleavage rest on incomplete evidence, with key experiments relying on phospho-mimetic proxies, a non-astrocyte overexpression system, and a single patient-derived iPSC line.

**Summary**
The authors investigate how disease-associated GFAP mutations lead to defective GFAP proteostasis in Alexander disease (AxD), a fatal neurological disorder caused by dominant gain-of-function GFAP mutations. Using phospho-proteomics on post-mortem brain tissue from 13 AxD patients and controls, they identify Ser13 phosphorylation as selectively elevated in patients who died very young (the most severe, infantile form). They validate this finding with a phospho-specific antibody and demonstrate that phospho-mimetic mutants (S13D, S13E) form aggregates in SW13vim⁻ cells and fail to assemble filaments in vitro. Using CRISPR-corrected isogenic iPSC-derived astrocytes from one AxD patient (R239C), they show pSer13-GFAP localizes to perinuclear aggregates associated with nuclear invaginations. They further report that caspase-6 expression and GFAP cleavage at Asp225 are elevated in young AxD patients and correlate with Ser13 phosphorylation, proposing a PTM signature of severe AxD.

**Strengths**
- The translational approach — beginning with unbiased phospho-proteomics on human post-mortem brain tissue and validating with orthogonal methods (mass spectrometry, phospho-specific antibody immunoblotting, immunofluorescence) — is well-conceived and yields a clinically meaningful observation about a disease severity biomarker.
- Generation of isogenic iPSC-astrocytes using CRISPR/Cas9 correction of the patient mutation, with appropriate CRISPR controls (edited on the WT allele), off-target analysis, karyotyping, and pluripotency characterization represents a rigorous disease modeling strategy.
- The in vitro assembly experiments with purified GFAP (Figure 3C) provide clean biophysical evidence that the phosphorylation status of Ser13 is critical for GFAP filament formation.
- The observation that perinuclear GFAP aggregates in iPSC-astrocytes recapitulate key features of Rosenthal fibers (DAPI-positive cores, GFAP-positive periphery, association with nuclear invaginations) adds confidence that this model captures disease-relevant biology.
- The paper is refreshingly transparent about its limitations, explicitly stating that causality between phosphorylation and caspase cleavage has not been established.

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**
- **Single patient iPSC line**: All iPSC-astrocyte experiments derive from a single AxD patient (R239C). Given the heterogeneity of AxD mutations (>70 residues), conclusions about AxD-general mechanisms from one mutation are limited. At minimum, the authors should discuss this limitation more prominently and ideally include at least one additional mutation in the iPSC model.
- **Confound between age and disease severity**: The dichotomization of patients into "young" (died 0.5–14 years) and "old" (died 27–50 years) conflates developmental stage, disease duration, and disease severity. Post-mortem interval, cause of death, and treatment history are not discussed as potential confounders for phosphorylation or caspase-6 levels. The authors should provide and discuss post-mortem interval data and address whether phosphorylation could be a post-mortem artifact or an age-of-brain-development effect.
- **Phospho-mimetic limitations**: Aspartate/glutamate substitutions are imperfect phosphoserine mimics (single vs. double negative charge, different geometry). Critically, purified S13D GFAP forms homogeneous globular structures that are *not* aggregation-prone in vitro (Figure 3C), yet forms large aggregates in cells — this discrepancy is not adequately addressed. It suggests that aggregation requires cellular co-factors or that the phospho-mimic phenotype in cells may not faithfully reflect true phosphorylation.
- **Mass spectrometry on HSE fraction only**: Analyzing only the insoluble high-salt extract introduces a selection bias toward GFAP in aggregated/filamentous states. Phosphorylation on soluble GFAP pools is not captured, making it impossible to determine whether pSer13 is enriched specifically on aggregated GFAP or is globally elevated. The authors should discuss this limitation and ideally analyze soluble fractions as well.
- **Caspase-6 mechanistic link is weak**: The D225E cleavage-resistant mutant itself causes significant filament bundling and perinuclear structures (Figure 8C-D), confounding interpretation. The caspase-6 inhibitor reduces aggregate size and hmm-GFAP but does not resolve aggregation (Figure 8E-G). Together, these experiments do not support a clean model in which caspase-6 cleavage drives aggregation. The relationship between phosphorylation and caspase-6 remains entirely correlative.
- **SW13vim⁻ overexpression system**: This adrenal carcinoma cell line lacks endogenous IF networks and the signaling environment of astrocytes. The authors appropriately note this caveat but still draw mechanistic conclusions (phosphorylation→cleavage→aggregation) primarily from this system. Key findings (e.g., S16/S17 phosphorylation on R79H, caspase-6 inhibitor effects) should be validated in iPSC-astrocytes.
- **pSer13 in isogenic controls**: The detection of pSer13-GFAP in isogenic control iPSC-astrocytes (Figure 6A) undermines the specificity of this modification as a disease marker. The authors attribute this to culture conditions but do not investigate further. This is a significant concern that should be addressed experimentally (e.g., by comparing freshly plated vs. stressed cultures, or by quantifying pSer13 levels biochemically in isogenic vs. AxD iPSC-astrocytes).
- **Statistical concerns**: Several key comparisons involve very small sample sizes (n=3 biological replicates for some cell-based experiments; 8 vs. 5 patients for the young vs. old comparison). The use of unpaired t-tests and ANOVAs with such small n values, without reporting effect sizes or confidence intervals, limits confidence. The notable outliers in Figure 2B (lanes 3 and 11) are acknowledged but not explained — were these patients clinically distinct?
- **No kinase identification**: While candidate kinases are listed (Supplementary file 4), no functional experiments with kinase inhibitors or activators were performed. Demonstrating that inhibition of a specific kinase reduces pSer13 in AxD astrocytes would substantially strengthen the translational relevance.

**Minor Comments**
- Figure 2D shows 12 non-AxD controls for immunoblot but only 3 were used for mass spectrometry (Supplementary file 2 lists non-AxD controls but the text is ambiguous about which were used for which assay). This should be clarified.
- The claim that caspase-6 is "essentially undetectable" in older AxD patients (Figure 9A) appears to be based on a single immunoblot. Quantification and statistical testing of this blot should be provided.
- Figure 7E: Different amounts of total protein were loaded to normalize GFAP monomer levels, which is unconventional. A standard loading with separate normalization would be more transparent.
- The electron microscopy of iPSC-astrocytes (Figure 6B) shows filamentous bundles but not electron-dense RFs. The authors note this but could more clearly discuss what stage of RF formation these structures might represent.
- Several supplementary files are referenced (Supplementary files 1–4) but their content is only partially described in the text. Complete legends for all supplementary materials should be provided.
- The title or abstract should more explicitly convey that the study is primarily correlative/descriptive rather than mechanistic.

**Recommendation**
Major Revisions
The study presents a potentially important disease biomarker finding (pSer13-GFAP and caspase-6 in severe AxD), but the mechanistic claims exceed what the current evidence supports, the iPSC model relies on a single patient line, and several key controls and quantifications are needed to meet eLife's standards for rigor and reproducibility.
