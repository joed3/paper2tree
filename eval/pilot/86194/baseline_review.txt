**eLife Assessment**
This valuable study provides solid evidence linking lysosomal lipid homeostasis to endogenous protein aggregation via ESCRT pathway disruption, though the central mechanistic claim connecting lipid accumulation to ESCRT dysfunction and subsequent proteostasis impairment rests on incomplete evidence that would benefit from direct functional assays of ESCRT-dependent processes.

**Summary**
The authors perform genome-wide CRISPRi screens in K562 cells using the ProteoStat aggresome dye to identify genetic modifiers of endogenous protein aggregation. Beyond expected proteostasis nodes (proteasome, vATPases, ribosomal subunits), the screens reveal a prominent role for lysosomal lipid homeostasis—particularly sphingomyelin (SM) and cholesterol ester (CE) metabolism—in regulating protein aggregation. Lipidomics confirms that SM and CE levels correlate with aggregation phenotypes across lipid-modifying perturbations. Lyso-IP proteomics identifies accumulation of detergent-insoluble ESCRT pathway components in lysosomes upon MYLIP knockdown (which upregulates LDLR and lipid uptake). The authors systematically rule out several alternative mechanisms—lysosomal pH changes, protease impairment, proteasome inhibition, and lipid peroxidation—and demonstrate that lysosomal membrane permeabilization (LMP) contributes partially to the phenotype. The work proposes a model in which excess SM/CE disrupts ESCRT-mediated membrane remodeling at lysosomes, impairing microautophagy and membrane repair, leading to aggregate accumulation.

**Strengths**
- The genome-wide, unbiased screening approach applied to endogenous protein aggregation (without overexpression of disease-associated proteins or exogenous seeds) is a significant conceptual advance and fills an important gap in the proteostasis field.
- The epistasis experiments (MYLIP/LDLR double KD, MYLIP/PSAP double KD, delipidated serum experiments) provide convincing genetic evidence that the effect on proteostasis is mediated through lipid uptake and metabolism rather than off-target activities.
- The lipidomics analysis linking specific lipid classes (SM and CE) to ProteoStat phenotype, with appropriate statistical framework (R² distribution comparisons using K-S tests), is well-executed and informative.
- The Lyso-IP proteomics revealing ESCRT component insolubility is a novel and striking finding, particularly the specificity of enrichment in insoluble versus total lysosomal fractions (Figure 4—figure supplement 1A), which argues against trivial accumulation.
- Systematic interrogation and elimination of multiple alternative mechanisms (lysosomal pH via pHLARE, protease activity via DQ-Red BSA, proteasome activity via Me4BoVS, lipid peroxidation via antioxidants) strengthens the ESCRT-centric model.
- The PSAP modular rescue experiments leveraging disease-associated mutations are creative and support the conclusion that proteostasis impairment results from pleiotropic sphingolipid accumulation rather than a single species.

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**
- **ESCRT dysfunction is inferred but not directly demonstrated.** The central claim that lipid dysregulation disrupts ESCRT function at lysosomes rests on accumulation of detergent-insoluble ESCRT-III components. However, ESCRT-III subunits form detergent-resistant polymers as part of their normal functional cycle. The authors should provide direct evidence of impaired ESCRT-dependent processes, such as quantification of intraluminal vesicle (ILV) formation by electron microscopy, measurement of MVB biogenesis, or functional microautophagy assays (e.g., using fluorescent reporters of microautophagic flux). Without such evidence, the insolubility data are consistent with—but do not prove—ESCRT dysfunction.

- **The VPS4A overexpression experiment (Figure 5D–E) yields ambiguous results.** VPS4A rescues chemically induced LMP but not MYLIP KD-induced LMP, and only modestly reduces lysosomal protein aggregation. The authors interpret this as lipid dysregulation acting "upstream or orthogonal to ESCRT-III resolution," but this could equally suggest that the ESCRT pathway is not the primary mediator. The authors should consider testing whether ESCRT-III KD is epistatic to MYLIP KD for the aggregation phenotype, which would more directly test the proposed pathway.

- **Proteasome activity data (Figure 6G–H) are from n=1 experiments.** Given that proteasome impairment by CE accumulation has been reported in the literature (van der Kant et al., 2019), and the authors explicitly address this claim, biological replicates with statistical analysis are essential. The same applies to Figure 6—figure supplement 1E.

- **K562 cell relevance and generalizability.** K562 cells are rapidly dividing leukemia cells with potentially atypical lipid metabolism and proteostasis. The authors acknowledge the absence of lipofuscin (unlike iPSC-derived neurons), and the null result for macroautophagy genes is notable. Key findings—particularly the ESCRT insolubility and LMP phenotypes—should be validated in at least one additional cell type, ideally a post-mitotic or disease-relevant cell (e.g., iPSC-derived neurons or primary macrophages, given the LDL uptake biology).

- **ProteoStat specificity concerns.** While the authors perform several controls (MBCD extraction, GUV experiments, Thioflavin T co-staining), ProteoStat is the primary readout for nearly all experiments. The possibility that ProteoStat may report on lipid-protein mixed aggregates, lipid-rich lysosomal storage material, or altered lysosomal microenvironment (rather than canonical amyloid-like protein aggregates) is not fully excluded. The Lyso-IP silver stain (Figure 3C) shows increased insoluble protein, but characterization of whether these are amyloid-like structures (e.g., by filter trap assay, SDD-AGE, or amyloid-specific antibodies such as OC or A11) would strengthen the claim.

- **The identity of aggregating proteins is not addressed.** The Lyso-IP proteomics identifies ESCRT components as becoming insoluble, but the broader composition of the lysosomal insoluble fraction—i.e., which endogenous proteins are aggregating—is not discussed. Are these known aggregation-prone proteins? Are they enriched for specific features (e.g., intrinsically disordered regions, metastable supersaturated proteins)? This information would significantly enhance the biological interpretation.

- **The correlation between SM/CE levels and ProteoStat (Figure 2E) is based on a limited number of genetic perturbations.** The core set includes only ~5–6 lipid-related perturbations, and some regression lines appear heavily influenced by single data points (e.g., the MYLIP KD point for CEs). The authors should discuss the robustness of these correlations and ideally include dose-response experiments with exogenous lipids to establish a more continuous relationship.

**Minor Comments**
- The GSEA scoring formula in the Methods conflates effect size and statistical confidence in a non-standard way. The rationale for this combined score should be justified, and sensitivity to this choice assessed.
- Figure 3C (silver stain) would benefit from quantification across replicates rather than showing a single representative gel.
- The manuscript would benefit from a clearer schematic of the proposed mechanistic model earlier in the Results, rather than only in Figure 7 (which is referenced but not shown in the provided text).
- The claim that macroautophagy plays a "negligible role" in clearing endogenous aggregates is strong given it is based on a single cell type under nutrient-replete conditions. This should be tempered.
- Several figure supplements are referenced but the descriptions in the text are sometimes difficult to follow without the actual figures. Cross-referencing could be improved.
- The paper would benefit from discussion of how findings relate to Niemann-Pick Type C models, where cholesterol/sphingolipid storage and ESCRT dysfunction have been independently studied.
- Timepoints for CRISPRi knockdown (6–7 days) should be justified more explicitly—are these sufficient for full protein depletion of all targets?

**Recommendation**
Major Revisions
The study addresses an important question and presents a novel connection between lipid metabolism and proteostasis via the ESCRT pathway, but the central mechanistic claim requires direct functional evidence of ESCRT impairment, key experiments need biological replication, and generalizability beyond K562 cells should be established.
