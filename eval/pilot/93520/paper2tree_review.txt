**eLife Assessment**

This useful study provides solid evidence that experimental evolution can partially recover growth fitness lost through genome reduction in *E. coli*, but the central conceptual claims regarding transcriptome homeostasis and a novel "divergent to convergent" evolutionary strategy rest on incomplete evidence, with key analyses undermined by insufficient statistical power and overinterpretation of conserved structural features.

**Summary**

The authors performed experimental evolution of a genome-reduced *E. coli* strain (~21% smaller genome) across nine independent lineages for approximately 1000 generations in minimal medium. They report that eight of nine lineages significantly improved growth rate, accumulated diverse mutations (65 total, with no position-level overlap), and maintained a conserved six-period chromosomal periodicity in their transcriptomes despite hundreds of differentially expressed genes per lineage. Using WGCNA, the authors identify three gene modules they associate with growth fitness, transcriptional reorganization, and mutagenesis, respectively. The paper's central thesis is that multiple evolutionary paths—diverse in both mutations and gene expression changes—converge on improved growth fitness while maintaining a "homeostatic transcriptome architecture," representing a novel "divergent to convergent" evolutionary strategy distinct from classical parallel or divergent evolution.

The experimental design is well-conceived, with nine independent lineages providing meaningful replication for the evolutionary experiment. However, the paper's most ambitious conceptual claims are inadequately supported by the analyses presented, with several key conclusions resting on underpowered statistics, overinterpretation of potentially trivial observations, and conflation of correlation with causation.

**Strengths**

- The demonstration that experimental evolution compensates for genome reduction-mediated growth loss is robustly supported: eight of nine lineages show statistically significant growth rate improvement (Mann-Whitney U test), with clear quantitative data from biological replicates (N=4–6) and consistent results across independent lineages (high-support claim c1.1).

- The comprehensive mutation catalog (65 mutations across nine lineages, documented via Breseq pipeline) and the finding that the nonsynonymous SNP ratio is significantly higher than expected under neutrality (36/45, p<0.01) provide convincing evidence that the accumulated mutations are adaptive rather than neutral (high-support claim c1.2.5).

- The enrichment of essential genes among mutated genes (22% vs. 9% genome-wide; chi-square p=0.008) is a biologically interesting and well-supported observation that speaks to the evolutionary constraints operating on the reduced genome (high-support claim c1.2.4).

- Fourier transform analysis with Fisher's g test rigorously demonstrates conservation of six-period chromosomal periodicity across all conditions (WT, ancestor, nine Evos), and the consistency of this finding across 11+ conditions is noteworthy (high-support claim c1.3).

- The observation that evolved transcriptomes converge toward each other but diverge from wild-type—supported by both hierarchical clustering and PCA—is clearly demonstrated and biologically important, establishing that evolution does not simply reinstate the ancestral expression state (high-support claim c1.3.4).

- The systematic comparison of genome reduction-mediated vs. evolution-mediated DEGs, using multiple enrichment approaches (regulon, GO, KEGG), consistently showing low overlap, provides a principled demonstration that the transcriptomic responses to these two perturbations are largely distinct (high-support claim c1.4).

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**

- **The concept of "transcriptome homeostasis" is defined almost entirely by conservation of chromosomal six-period periodicity, which is likely a trivially conserved structural feature.** The six-period pattern likely reflects the physical architecture of the *E. coli* chromosome (macrodomains), and its conservation may be expected regardless of evolutionary changes. Meanwhile, the actual gene expression patterns diverge substantially (333–1130 DEGs per lineage), which directly contradicts the homeostasis framing. The authors must either provide evidence that periodicity conservation is non-trivial (e.g., showing conditions where it breaks down) or substantially temper this claim (low-support aspects of c1.3 and c1.6.4).

- **The WGCNA analysis is critically underpowered with only N=9 evolved samples.** Standard recommendations for WGCNA require substantially more samples for reliable module-trait correlations. The claims that Module M2 is "responsible for" growth fitness, M10 for transcriptional reorganization, and M16 for mutagenesis (claims c1.5.1, c1.5.2, c1.5.3) conflate correlation with causation and rest on statistically unreliable foundations. The actual correlation coefficients and corrected p-values are not reported in the main text. The authors should either validate these modules with independent data, perform sensitivity analyses, or present these findings as purely exploratory with appropriate caveats. As written, this analysis does not meet eLife's evidence standards.

- **The proposed "divergent to convergent" evolutionary strategy (claim c1.6) is presented as novel but is well-documented in microbial experimental evolution.** Convergent phenotypic evolution via diverse genetic changes is a hallmark of microbial evolution experiments (e.g., Tenaillon et al. 2012, *Science*; Barrick & Lenski 2013, *Genome Biology*). The authors' own data also show elements of parallel evolution (insH deletions, *rpo* gene mutations in three lineages, amino acid metabolism enrichment), blurring the distinction from classical parallel evolution. The novelty claim needs to be substantially revised with proper contextualization of the extensive existing literature.

- **The claim that "no common mutagenetic strategy" exists across lineages (claim c1.2) uses an overly narrow criterion—position-level overlap—to define commonality.** The frequent insH deletions, *rpo* mutations in three lineages, and convergent enrichment of amino acid metabolism pathways all suggest functional convergence that the authors understate. A more nuanced analysis considering gene-level, operon-level, and pathway-level convergence is needed. With only 65 mutations across nine lineages, the statistical power to detect commonality is inherently limited.

- **The r/K trade-off framing is internally contradictory.** The paper claims a "trade-off in population size" (c1.1) while simultaneously reporting a positive correlation between r and K among evolved populations (c1.1.2) and speculating that carrying capacity decline is neutral drift (c1.1.4). These contradictory framings need to be reconciled. The authors should either present evidence distinguishing trade-off from drift or avoid the trade-off terminology.

- **Population-level sequencing rather than clonal isolates introduces uncertainty.** Mutations identified from stationary-phase population samples may not be truly fixed, and growth rate measurements may reflect population heterogeneity rather than single-genotype properties. This limitation should be explicitly discussed and its implications for the central claims evaluated.

- **The claim that gene modules represent "a biologically meaningful alternative genetic classification" (c1.5.5) is entirely speculative.** WGCNA modules are condition-specific and derived from only nine samples; they cannot serve as a general classification system. Similarly, the claims about fitness landscape ruggedness (c1.6.2) and equivalence of genome reduction to stress response (c1.6.3) lack supporting data and should be removed or clearly labeled as speculation.

- **The random simulation for locational bias (c1.2.3) yields a borderline result (μp = 0.07–0.09) that is presented as non-significant but could equally be interpreted as a trend toward locational bias.** The use of mean p-values from 1000 simulations is an unconventional statistical approach; the full distribution of p-values should be shown and a more standard permutation-based framework employed.

**Minor Comments**

- The actual p-values for growth rate comparisons should be stated in the text, not only in figure legends.

- The percentage of variance explained by PC1 and PC2 in the PCA analysis should be reported.

- The number of biological replicates varies across conditions (N=2–4 for RNA-seq); the impact of this variation on DEG calling sensitivity should be discussed.

- The ninth non-significant lineage (for growth improvement) warrants specific discussion—understanding why it failed would strengthen the overall narrative.

- Figure 7 illustrating the three evolutionary strategies, while visually clear, oversimplifies the distinction between categories that are likely points on a continuum rather than discrete types.

- The histidine pathway enrichment in common DEGs is noted but not mechanistically discussed—given its prominence as the only commonly enriched pathway, this deserves elaboration.

- The paper would benefit from explicitly stating the Spearman correlation coefficient and p-value for the r-K relationship in the main text.

**Recommendation**

Major Revisions

The core experimental evolution results and basic genomic/transcriptomic characterization are solid (24 high-support claims), but 11 claims receive low support, and several of these underpin the paper's most prominent conceptual contributions—particularly the WGCNA-based functional module analysis, the "divergent to convergent" evolutionary framework, and the transcriptome homeostasis concept. These issues require substantial revision, additional statistical validation, and more measured interpretation before the paper meets eLife's standards for a conceptual advance.
