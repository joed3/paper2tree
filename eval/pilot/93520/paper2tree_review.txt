**eLife Assessment**

This study presents a **useful** investigation of how genome-reduced *E. coli* recovers growth fitness through experimental evolution, combining genomic, transcriptomic, and network analyses across nine independent lineages. While the phenotypic and descriptive genomic/transcriptomic data provide **solid** evidence for growth recovery and mutation diversity, several central interpretive claims — particularly the gene network module analysis, the proposed novel evolutionary strategy, and the concept of transcriptome homeostasis — rest on **incomplete** to **inadequate** evidence, substantially weakening the paper's conceptual contribution.

**Summary**

This paper investigates whether experimental evolution can compensate for the growth fitness loss caused by large-scale genome reduction in *Escherichia coli*. Nine independent lineages of a genome-reduced strain were evolved for approximately 1,000 generations via serial transfer in minimal medium. The authors report that eight of nine lineages significantly recovered growth rate, that the 65 fixed mutations were highly diverse across lineages with no positional overlap, and that transcriptomes reorganized in a common direction (away from wild-type) while conserving a six-period chromosomal periodicity pattern. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was used to identify three gene modules proposed to be separately responsible for growth fitness, transcriptional reorganization, and mutation accumulation. The authors frame their findings as evidence for a novel "divergent-to-convergent" evolutionary strategy, distinct from classical parallel or divergent evolution, in which diverse mutations converge on a homeostatic transcriptome architecture.

The experimental evolution and descriptive characterization are competently executed, but the paper's most ambitious claims — the WGCNA-based functional module assignments, the novelty of the evolutionary strategy, and the biological significance of transcriptome periodicity as "homeostasis" — are inadequately supported and, in some cases, internally inconsistent.

**Strengths**

- **Growth recovery is robustly demonstrated (c1.1, c1.1.1):** Eight of nine independently evolved lineages showed statistically significant growth rate improvement (Mann-Whitney U test, N=4–6 replicates), providing strong quantitative evidence that experimental evolution can compensate for genome reduction-mediated fitness loss. The nine-lineage design with biological replication is a clear strength.

- **Mutation characterization is thorough (c1.2.1, c1.2.4, c1.2.5):** Complete genome resequencing identified 65 mutations catalogued in full, with a significantly elevated nonsynonymous/synonymous ratio (36/45, p<0.01) and enrichment of essential genes among mutated genes (11/49 vs. 286/3290, χ² p=0.008), providing credible evidence for positive selection rather than neutral drift.

- **Six-period chromosomal periodicity is consistently conserved (c1.3, c1.3.1):** Fourier transform analysis with Fisher's g test confirmed statistically significant six-period periodicity across all nine evolved transcriptomes, the ancestor, and wild-type, providing a reproducible quantitative observation documented in Supplementary file 5.

- **DEG diversity across lineages is clearly demonstrated (c1.3.3):** Only 108 DEGs were common across all nine lineages out of 333–1,130 per lineage, with declining overlap as more lineages are considered (UpSet plot, Figure 4A-B). This convincingly shows that transcriptomic changes are largely lineage-specific.

- **Evolved transcriptomes converge toward each other but away from wild-type (c1.3.4):** Both hierarchical clustering and PCA independently show that evolved transcriptomes cluster together but separately from wild-type, clearly demonstrating that evolution does not simply reinstate the ancestral expression program.

- **Genome reduction and evolution DEGs show low functional overlap (c1.4.2, c1.4.3):** Multiple enrichment approaches (regulon, GO, KEGG) and an alternative DEG method (RankProd) consistently show poor overlap between genome reduction-mediated and evolution-mediated expression changes, supporting the claim that these represent distinct transcriptional responses.

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**

- **WGCNA analysis is critically underpowered (c1.5, c1.5.1, c1.5.2, c1.5.3):** The WGCNA was performed with only ~9 evolved samples, far below the minimum recommended for reliable module-trait correlations. With 21 modules and three traits, the 63 comparisons carry a substantial false discovery risk at this sample size. The actual correlation coefficients and FDR-corrected p-values for module-trait associations are not reported in the text. No sensitivity analysis for parameter choices (soft threshold = 12, merge cut height = 0.25) or module stability (e.g., leave-one-out) is provided. The claim that modules M2, M10, and M16 are "responsible for" growth, transcription, and mutagenesis conflates correlation with causation. Furthermore, the functional labels assigned in the Discussion (replication, transcription, population dynamics) do not match the actual enrichment results (iron transport, transposition, translation). This analysis requires either substantially more samples or should be presented as exploratory rather than as a primary finding.

- **The "divergent-to-convergent" evolutionary strategy is not novel (c1.6, c1.6.1):** Diverse genotypes converging on similar phenotypes is a well-documented phenomenon in microbial experimental evolution. The paper does not engage with the extensive literature on convergent evolution via diverse mutations (e.g., Tenaillon et al. 2012, *Science*; Barrick & Lenski 2013). The paper's own data show parallel features (insH deletions in multiple lineages, rpo mutations in three lineages, amino acid metabolism enrichment) that blur the distinction from parallel evolution. No formal statistical test is presented to distinguish this pattern from known cases of parallel or convergent evolution. The three-category framework (Figure 7) overstates novelty and should be contextualized within existing evolutionary theory.

- **"Transcriptome homeostasis" is inadequately defined and may be trivially conserved (c1.3, root claim):** The concept of transcriptome homeostasis rests almost entirely on conservation of chromosomal six-period periodicity, which likely reflects the invariant physical macrodomain structure of the *E. coli* chromosome. The paper does not demonstrate any condition under which this periodicity is disrupted, which would be necessary to make its conservation noteworthy. The 100 kb moving average used in Fourier analysis is extremely coarse and would smooth over local changes while trivially preserving large-scale patterns. Meanwhile, actual gene expression levels change dramatically (333–1,130 DEGs per lineage), undermining the "homeostasis" label. The authors should either demonstrate that periodicity can be disrupted under some conditions or substantially temper this claim.

- **No causal link between specific mutations and fitness recovery is established (root claim weakness):** Despite identifying 65 mutations, the paper does not demonstrate that any individual mutation is adaptive through reconstruction experiments, allelic replacement, or even computational prediction of functional consequences. The claim that mutations represent "evolutionary paths" to fitness recovery requires at minimum some causal validation. The lack of correlation between mutation number and fitness increase (c1.2.2, p>0.1 with N=9) could reflect low statistical power rather than true independence.

- **The r/K trade-off framing is internally inconsistent (c1.1, c1.1.2, c1.1.4):** The paper simultaneously claims a "trade-off in carrying capacity" (c1.1), reports a positive correlation between growth rate and carrying capacity among evolved populations (c1.1.2), and speculates that carrying capacity decline is neutral drift (c1.1.4). These three statements are contradictory. The universal decline in carrying capacity across all nine lineages is unlikely to be drift if population sizes are large, and may instead reflect antagonistic pleiotropy or serial transfer protocol effects. This inconsistency should be resolved.

- **Locational bias analysis is borderline (c1.2.3):** The mean p-value from 1,000 random simulations (μp = 0.07–0.09) falls in a borderline range that many biologists would consider marginally significant. Reporting this as "no locational bias" is an overstatement. Furthermore, using the mean of p-values as a summary statistic is unconventional; the distribution of p-values or a permutation-based approach would be more appropriate.

- **Claims equating genome reduction and evolution to stress responses are unsupported (c1.6.3):** The assertion that "genome reduction and evolution are equivalent to stress responses" based on shared mobile element and flagella-related gene functions lacks direct experimental evidence. No stress markers were measured. Flagellar assembly was enriched only in genome reduction DEGs and absent in all evolved populations, directly contradicting shared stress response. This claim should be removed or substantially revised.

- **The gene module classification claim is unsubstantiated (c1.5.4):** The suggestion that WGCNA modules represent "a biologically meaningful alternative genetic classification besides commonly used criteria" is entirely speculative, with no validation, no comparison to existing classifications, and no evidence of stability or generalizability beyond this specific experimental context.

**Minor Comments**

- The actual p-values for growth rate comparisons in individual lineages should be reported in the main text, not only in figures. Similarly, the Spearman correlation coefficient for the r-K relationship (c1.1.2) should be stated explicitly.

- The percentage of variance explained by PC1 and PC2 in the PCA analysis (c1.3.4) should be reported to allow readers to assess how much of the transcriptomic variation is captured.

- The one lineage that did not show significant growth improvement deserves discussion — understanding why it failed could provide mechanistic insight.

- The claim that rpo gene mutations "participate in global regulation for improved growth" (c1.2.6) should be explicitly labeled as a hypothesis, as no experimental validation was performed. rpo mutations are common in many experimental evolution contexts and may not be specific to genome reduction compensation.

- Figure 7's three-category evolutionary strategy illustration would benefit from citations to the existing literature on convergent and parallel evolution to properly position the findings.

- The number of biological replicates varies across lineages (N=2–4 for transcriptomics), which may introduce differential statistical power for DEG calling; this limitation should be acknowledged.

**Recommendation**

Major Revisions

The descriptive genomic and transcriptomic data are solid and potentially valuable to the experimental evolution community, but three of six primary claims have low support (WGCNA modules, novel evolutionary strategy, transcriptome homeostasis), and these form the paper's central conceptual contribution. The WGCNA analysis requires either additional samples or substantial re-framing as exploratory, the novelty claims must be contextualized within existing literature on convergent evolution, and the transcriptome homeostasis concept requires stronger justification or tempering before the paper meets eLife's standards for conceptual advance.
