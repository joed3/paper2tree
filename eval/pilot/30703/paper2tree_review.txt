**eLife Assessment**
This valuable study presents solid evidence that wound-induced ROS signaling regulates vimentin expression and collagen reorganization during zebrafish caudal fin regeneration, but the central mechanistic claim that collagen projections physically guide epithelial extensions rests on incomplete evidence, and several key pharmacological experiments are confounded by drug non-specificity and toxicity.

**Summary**
LeBert et al. investigate how wound-associated reactive oxygen species (ROS) regulate tissue regeneration in the zebrafish larval caudal fin. The authors generate a vimentin promoter reporter line, Tg(−2vim:egfp), and use it to identify a population of non-migratory vimentin-positive cells that appear at the wound margin in a ROS- and NFκB-dependent manner. Using morpholino knockdown, CRISPR F0 mosaics, pharmacological inhibition, and advanced imaging (SHG, LC-PolScope, multiphoton time-lapse), the authors propose a signaling chain: wound-induced ROS → NFκB → vimentin expression → collagen production and reorganization → epithelial projections → regenerative outgrowth. They further use burn wounds and collagen cross-linking inhibition (BAPN) to argue that collagen fiber integrity is essential for epithelial projection formation and efficient healing. The study represents an ambitious effort to link early redox signaling to the physical mechanics of wound repair.

**Strengths**
- The demonstration that vimentin is required for fin regeneration is robustly supported by convergent evidence from two independent morpholinos, RNA rescue, and CRISPR F0 mosaics, with large sample sizes (n=85–130 per group) and high statistical significance (p<0.0001), while developmental controls confirm wound-specificity (claims c1.3, c1.3.1, c1.3.3, c1.3.4).
- The Tg(−2vim:egfp) reporter line is well-validated against published in situ hybridization data and responds appropriately to EMT induction via oncogenic Ras, establishing it as a meaningful tool for tracking vimentin promoter activity in vivo.
- The multi-modal imaging approach—combining SHG, LC-PolScope, bright field, phalloidin staining, and live fluorescence—provides compelling visualization of collagen fiber-epithelial cell associations within wound-edge projections (claims c1.5.3, c1.5.4). The technical achievement of long-term dual SHG/fluorescence imaging in living larvae is noteworthy.
- Quantitative SHG analyses using CurveAlign for fiber orientation and contour length measurements provide objective, reproducible metrics demonstrating that vimentin depletion reduces fiber projections and alters fiber alignment at the wound edge (claims c1.6.1, c1.6.2), with good sample sizes and multiple replicates.
- The qRT-PCR data showing reduced col1a1 and col2a1b expression in vimentin-depleted larvae (claim c1.6.3) provides molecular evidence linking vimentin to collagen regulation, consistent with and extending prior in vitro findings from mammalian systems.
- The observation that wound-induced vim+ cells are non-migratory and arise locally at the wound edge is a genuinely surprising finding, well-documented by time-lapse imaging (claim c1.2.2).

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**
- **NFκB pathway claim is critically undermined by drug non-specificity (claim c1.2.4, low support).** The authors rely exclusively on Withaferin A (WA) to implicate NFκB in regulating vimentin promoter activity, yet they acknowledge that WA directly binds and inhibits vimentin protein. This creates an irresolvable confound: the observed loss of reporter signal could reflect NFκB-dependent transcriptional regulation, direct vimentin protein destabilization, or both. A genetic approach to NFκB perturbation (e.g., IκB super-repressor, dominant-negative p65, or p65 morpholino) is essential to support this claim. Without such validation, NFκB should be removed from the proposed pathway or clearly marked as speculative.

- **The central claim that collagen projections "guide" or "push" epithelial cells lacks direct causal evidence (claim c1.5, medium support).** The temporal and spatial association between extending SHG fibers and epithelial cell projections is demonstrated by impressive live imaging, but the interpretation that fibers mechanically drive cell extension is not tested. The data are equally consistent with epithelial cells actively migrating and passively deforming or depositing collagen. Laser ablation of individual collagen fibers during live imaging, or quantitative analysis of which structure extends first (fiber vs. cell), would significantly strengthen this claim. At minimum, the authors should temper their language from "push" and "guide" to "associate with" and discuss alternative interpretations.

- **BAPN experiments are severely compromised by toxicity (claim c1.7.1, low support).** The authors acknowledge that BAPN causes lethality between 4–5 dpf, limiting analysis to 2 dpw. This toxicity makes it impossible to distinguish collagen cross-linking effects from generalized cellular harm. Furthermore, the contour length actually *increased* with BAPN treatment—opposite to the predicted direction—which the authors attribute to a measurement artifact but which undermines the quantification approach. The authors should either identify a non-toxic concentration/treatment window or substantially downweight this evidence in their conclusions.

- **Burn wound experiments conflate collagen loss with extensive tissue damage (claim c1.7.2, medium support).** Comparing burn wounds to surgical amputation introduces numerous confounding variables beyond collagen fiber integrity: thermal denaturation of proteins, cell death, vascular damage, and altered inflammatory responses. The claim that impaired healing after burns is attributable to collagen loss is not adequately supported. The authors should acknowledge these confounds more explicitly and avoid concluding that collagen loss per se drives the healing defect.

- **The identity of wound-induced vim+ cells remains unresolved (claim c1.2.3, low support).** The authors exclude epithelial cells and macrophages using limited markers (krt4, mpeg1, pu.1 MO) but cannot positively identify these cells. For a study centered on the function of vim+ cells, this is a significant gap. Additional characterization using fibroblast markers, single-cell approaches, or lineage tracing would substantially strengthen the narrative.

- **Projection scoring appears subjective and lacks rigor (claims c1.4.2, c1.4.3).** The scoring of "visible projections" as a binary outcome is not defined by explicit criteria, no blinding is mentioned, and the confidence intervals are extremely wide (e.g., vim MO 95% CI: 7.56–65.78%; DMSO 95% CI: 31.76–99.58%). This suggests enormous inter-replicate variability that undermines confidence in the effect sizes. The authors should define explicit, blinded scoring criteria or develop quantitative projection metrics (number, length, width).

- **DPI non-specificity weakens the ROS claims (claim c1.2.4).** DPI inhibits multiple flavin-containing enzymes beyond NADPH oxidases, including mitochondrial complex I and nitric oxide synthase. While Duox1 morpholino data partially addresses this, the Duox1 MO result is presented only qualitatively in a supplementary figure. Quantification comparable to the DPI experiment (fluorescence ratio analysis) should be provided for the Duox1 MO condition.

- **The morpholino does not affect the transgenic reporter, raising an unaddressed technical question (claim c1.3.5).** The authors show that vimentin MO does not block reporter EGFP expression, interpreting this as separable regulation. However, if the MO blocks translation via a 5'UTR or splice site mechanism, it should not affect the transgene (which lacks endogenous UTR sequences). This distinction is important and should be explicitly discussed; otherwise, the experiment is uninformative about separability.

**Minor Comments**
- The EMT validation experiment (HRasV12 vs. HRasG12) was performed in only two replicates with no quantification. While supportive, this should be acknowledged as preliminary.
- The Larp6 MO regeneration data comes from a single experiment in triplicate, limiting confidence in the partial phenocopy interpretation. This should be noted.
- Several in situ hybridization and SHG co-localization experiments were performed in only two replicates. While not uncommon for qualitative imaging, this should be stated transparently in the results.
- The paper would benefit from a clearer model figure summarizing the proposed pathway with explicit notation of which links are correlative versus causal.
- The claim of technical precedence for long-term in vivo SHG imaging (claim c1.5.5) is difficult to verify and could be softened further.
- Statistical reporting is inconsistent: some experiments use LSMeans analysis across replicates while others use paired t-tests on replicate percentages, making it difficult to evaluate the appropriateness of the statistical framework uniformly.

**Recommendation**
Major Revisions
The study addresses an important question and provides multiple lines of evidence supporting the ROS → vimentin → collagen axis, but several critical claims—particularly the NFκB involvement, the causal role of collagen in guiding epithelial projections, and the collagen disruption experiments (BAPN/burn)—rest on incomplete or confounded evidence that must be strengthened through genetic validation, quantitative analysis, and more rigorous controls before the paper meets eLife's standards for a mechanistic study.
