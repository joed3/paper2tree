**eLife Assessment**
This potentially valuable study investigates how hippocampal CA1 neurons encode delay and reward information during delay-discounting decisions in mice, but the central claims — particularly regarding value-coding neurons and functionally distinct delay-responsive populations — rest on incomplete evidence, with key confounds between spatial and temporal coding insufficiently addressed.

**Summary**
The authors recorded CA1 single-unit activity in mice performing a T-maze delay-discounting task in which animals chose between a small immediate reward and a large delayed reward. They identify two populations of neurons — "delay-active" and "delay-suppressed" — based on whether their firing rate increased or decreased during the delay period relative to the full trial. The paper reports that firing rates of a subset of these neurons correlate with delay length, that most carry spatial (side) selectivity, and that the two populations show opposing responses to reward size manipulations. From these findings, the authors propose that a subset of neurons may encode subjective value. They further demonstrate that CA1-specific NMDA receptor knockout mice show impaired delay discounting and disrupted delay-period neural coding. The study uses multiple behavioral manipulations (delay extension, position switching, both-side delays, reward loss/gain) and combines electrophysiology with genetic perturbation, aiming to identify hippocampal substrates for delay-based valuation.

**Strengths**
- The overall question — how hippocampal activity supports delay-based decision making — is important and relatively underexplored at the single-neuron level, representing a meaningful bridge between lesion studies and circuit-level understanding.
- The experimental design employs multiple, well-conceived behavioral manipulations (extension, switch, both-side, reward loss/gain conditions) to dissociate delay length, delay position, and reward size, which is a thoughtful approach to a multifactorial problem.
- The combination of electrophysiology with NMDAR conditional knockout mice provides a causal handle, and the finding that these mice show both behavioral impairment and disrupted delay-period neural activity strengthens the link between CA1 plasticity mechanisms and delay discounting.
- The dual-coding observation (both rate coding in individual neurons and population-level temporal coding) is well-documented and the SVM decoding analysis provides complementary evidence for population-level delay-length representation.
- The demonstration that reward manipulation produces opposing effects in delay-act versus delay-sup populations is an interesting dissociation that could have implications for understanding how the hippocampus contributes to cost-benefit valuation.

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**

- **Fundamental confound between spatial and delay coding.** The delay occurs at a fixed spatial location within the maze arm. Consequently, any place cell with a field in the delay zone would be classified as "delay-active," and any place cell with a field elsewhere would be "delay-suppressed." The remarkably high combined proportion (~87% of all recorded neurons classified as one or the other) and the finding that ~65–74% of these neurons are side-selective strongly suggest that the classification largely captures standard place cell properties rather than a specialized delay-coding population. The authors must provide a rigorous analysis distinguishing genuine delay/time modulation from spatial firing — for example, by comparing firing fields during delay versus non-delay traversals of the same location, or by analyzing activity only from neurons recorded in both-side conditions where spatial location is controlled.

- **The "value-coding" claim lacks statistical rigor.** The identification of value-coding neurons (Figure 7E) relies on visual inspection of a scatter plot to identify neurons "plotted around the line of delay effect = reward effect." No formal criterion, statistical test, or model comparison is applied to define or quantify this putative population. The authors themselves acknowledge "there are no global trends," yet still claim a subset encodes value. This central claim requires a principled analysis — e.g., a regression model testing whether individual neurons' delay-length sensitivity predicts their reward-size sensitivity, ideally with cross-validation.

- **Cell classification thresholds and inclusiveness.** The permutation-based classification of delay-act and delay-sup cells uses a 5% threshold in each tail, yet yields 38% and 49% of neurons, respectively. This suggests the test has extremely high power or that the baseline (whole-trial firing rate) is an inappropriate reference. The authors should clarify why nearly all neurons are classified as modulated and whether the results are robust to more conservative thresholds or alternative baseline periods (e.g., start zone only).

- **Sequential experimental design introduces order confounds.** Delays were always extended in a fixed order (0→5→10→20→40 s), meaning that delay length is confounded with time-in-session, satiety, and experience. Correlations between firing rate and delay length could therefore reflect fatigue, motivational decline, or cumulative learning rather than delay coding per se. At minimum, the authors should present analyses controlling for trial order or time-in-session, and ideally include sessions with randomized or decremented delay sequences.

- **NMDAR knockout interpretation is complicated by several issues.** (1) The authors acknowledge that Cre-mediated deletion likely extends beyond CA1 to CA3, dentate gyrus, and deep cortical layers at the ages used for recording, undermining the claim of CA1 specificity. (2) The opposite phenotype from hippocampal lesions (reduced rather than increased discounting) is acknowledged but unexplained, leaving the mechanistic interpretation ambiguous. (3) Basic properties such as running speed, trial completion rates, and motivation metrics are not reported for the cKO mice, making it unclear whether the behavioral and neural differences reflect delay-discounting deficits or more general impairments. (4) Sample sizes for cKO recordings (n=3 mice, 123 units) are modest and the comparison with controls (n=4, 69 units) is asymmetric.

- **Reward manipulation confounds with behavioral strategy.** When reward is decreased, animals shift their preference, meaning that on most trials the delayed arm is no longer chosen. The forced-choice manipulation attempts to address this but fundamentally changes the task structure. The authors should analyze trial-by-trial relationships between neural activity and choice on a within-session basis, and should report whether firing rate changes precede or follow the behavioral shift.

- **Insufficient distinction from time cells.** The relationship between the delay-active neurons described here and previously characterized hippocampal time cells (MacDonald et al., 2011; Pastalkova et al., 2008) is not clearly delineated. Are these the same phenomenon examined in a different task context, or do the authors claim a genuinely distinct population? A direct comparison or explicit analysis (e.g., temporal information content, sequence reliability) would clarify the novelty.

- **Low trial numbers and across-session comparisons.** With ~10 trials per condition per session and conditions tested across different weekly sessions, it is unclear how neurons were tracked or whether the same neurons contributed to multiple analyses. The authors should clarify tracking methodology and provide stability metrics (e.g., waveform similarity, cluster quality across sessions).

**Minor Comments**

- The manuscript would benefit from a clearer schematic or table summarizing which mice, sessions, and neuron counts contribute to each analysis, as the current presentation makes it difficult to follow the data flow.
- Figure 3 shows only single example neurons; additional examples or summary statistics in the main figures would strengthen the presentation.
- The LFP theta analysis (Figure 2C-D) is presented briefly and could be expanded — does theta power or frequency correlate with delay length or choice?
- The term "deformative normalization" (used to justify peak over mean firing rate analysis) is unclear and should be explained or rephrased.
- The Discussion's suggestion that the NMDAR knockout phenotype may relate to OCD-like compulsive behavior is speculative and should be tempered.
- Several figure panels lack axis labels or units; consistent formatting would improve readability.
- The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test for uniformity of peak firing time distributions should be reported with test statistics, not just p-values.

**Recommendation**
Major Revisions
The study addresses an interesting question and employs creative experimental manipulations, but the core claims about value coding and functionally distinct delay-responsive populations are not adequately distinguished from spatial coding confounds, and several key analyses lack the statistical rigor expected by eLife.
