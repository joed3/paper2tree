**eLife Assessment**

This study presents a **valuable** investigation into how hippocampal CA1 neurons encode delay and reward information during delay-discounting decisions, employing a creative multi-condition behavioral design and combining electrophysiology with genetic manipulation. However, the evidence is **incomplete** for several central claims — particularly those concerning value coding and subjective value representation — owing to critical statistical confounds in rate-coding analyses, post-hoc identification of value-coding neurons without formal criteria, and genetic manipulations that lack regional specificity.

**Summary**

This paper examines how hippocampal CA1 neurons contribute to delay-discounting decision making in mice. The authors recorded 639 CA1 units from 28 wildtype mice performing a T-maze task in which delay length, delay position, and reward amount were systematically varied across sessions. They identify two major subpopulations — delay-active (~38%) and delay-suppressed (~49%) neurons — and characterize their responses to delay length (via rate and population-level coding), spatial location (side-selectivity), and reward amount (opposing modulation patterns). The authors further report that conditional knockout of the NMDA receptor NR1 subunit in hippocampal pyramidal cells impairs delay-discounting behavior and disrupts delay-related neural activity. The central thesis is that these opposing neuronal subpopulations concertedly support a valuation process underlying delay-based decision making, potentially encoding subjective value.

The experimental design — incorporating extension, switch, both-side, reward-loss, and reward-gain conditions — is a notable strength, allowing partial dissociation of delay, spatial, and reward contributions. However, several of the paper's most ambitious claims, particularly regarding value coding and subjective value representation, rest on weak or confounded evidence, and the NMDAR knockout results are complicated by acknowledged regional non-specificity.

**Strengths**

- **Robust classification of delay-active and delay-suppressed populations (Primary Claim 1):** The identification of 243 delay-active and 313 delay-suppressed neurons from 639 recorded units, based on a permutation test with 1000 iterations, is well above chance expectations (~32 false positives per tail at 5% threshold). The classification is clearly quantified and illustrated with convincing single-neuron examples (Figure 3).

- **Strong SVM decoding of delay length from population activity (Supporting Claim, SVM decoding):** The decoding analysis yields a very large effect size (F₂ = 882.1, p < 0.001) validated against two types of shuffled controls and replicated across five animals, providing compelling evidence that CA1 populations carry information about delay length.

- **Clear behavioral impairment in cKO mice with delay-specific progression (Supporting Claim, cKO behavior):** The two-way ANOVA reveals a significant genotype effect (F₁ = 14.4, p < 0.001) and genotype × delay interaction (F₄ = 23.0, p < 0.001), with progressive divergence across delay levels (non-significant at 0 s, significant at 5–40 s). This delay-specific pattern strengthens the interpretation that the behavioral deficit relates to delay discounting rather than general cognitive impairment.

- **Opposing reward modulation of the two subpopulations (Supporting Claim, firing rate ratios):** Mann-Whitney U-tests show significant differences in firing rate ratios between delay-active and delay-suppressed cells under both reward loss (Z = −2.6, p = 0.007) and reward gain (Z = 2.1, p = 0.03), with one-sample t-tests confirming opposing skew directions (both p = 0.01). The bidirectional evidence across two conditions lends credibility.

- **Spatial tuning of delay-period neurons (Primary Claim 5):** Clear quantification that 73.5% of delay-active and 64.9% of delay-suppressed neurons show side-selectivity, with appropriate three-way ANOVA methodology and data from both switched and both-side conditions.

- **Transparent reporting of contradictions and limitations:** The authors forthrightly acknowledge the opposite phenotype compared to lesion studies, the potential extra-hippocampal spread of NR1 deletion, and the absence of a global trend linking delay and reward modulation — a commendable level of scientific transparency.

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**

- **Peak firing rate as a measure of delay-length rate coding is critically confounded (Primary Claim 2; Supporting Claims on rate coding):** The paper reports that 79.3% of delay-active and 72.3% of delay-suppressed neurons show significant peak firing rate correlations with delay length, but peak firing rate is mathematically expected to increase with longer observation windows even for a stationary Poisson process. This fundamental confound is not addressed or controlled for. The substantially lower mean firing rate correlations (34.5% and 37.3%) are consistent with substantial inflation of the peak rate measure. The authors must either demonstrate through simulation that the peak rate correlations exceed what is expected from the sampling duration confound, or rely solely on mean firing rate results. The related claim that peak firing rate is a "better indicator" than mean firing rate (labeled low support) should be retracted or supported with formal analysis.

- **Value-coding claim lacks formal statistical support (Primary Claim 4):** The identification of "value-coding neurons" near the diagonal of the scatter plot in Figure 7E is entirely visual and post hoc. No quantitative criterion, cluster analysis, or statistical test is applied to determine whether more neurons fall near the diagonal than expected by chance from independent delay and reward sensitivities. The authors themselves state "there are no global trends" and acknowledge that "further study will be required to isolate specific neurons encoding subjective value." This claim should either be removed or supported with a formal statistical framework (e.g., a permutation test for excess diagonal clustering, or model comparison between independent versus integrated coding).

- **Subjective value interpretation from forced-choice data is inadequately supported (Primary Claim 6; Supporting Claim on forced-choice):** The claim that delay-active neurons represent the animal's "subjective value" rests on individual cell examples from a forced-choice experiment without population-level quantification, formal statistical comparison between forced-rewarded and forced-unrewarded conditions, or reporting of sample sizes. The forced-choice design also fundamentally alters the task structure (no decision), and neuronal silence during forced unrewarded trials has multiple alternative explanations (absence of reward expectation, reduced motivation, behavioral disengagement) distinct from absence of subjective value.

- **NMDAR deletion lacks regional specificity for CA1-specific claims (Primary Claim 7; Evidence Claims on NR1 spread):** The authors acknowledge that NR1 deletion extends beyond CA1 to CA3, dentate gyrus, and deep cortical layers, particularly in the older animals used for physiology. Despite this acknowledged confound, the paper frames the NMDAR results as evidence for CA1-specific mechanisms. The physiological recordings from cKO animals (3 mice, 123 units) are particularly problematic given the age-dependent spread of deletion. The authors should either provide histological verification of deletion extent in recorded animals or substantially temper their interpretive claims.

- **Small sample sizes and marginal statistics for key physiological comparisons in cKO mice (Supporting Claims on cKO physiology):** The cKO physiological dataset comprises only 3 knockout and 4 control mice (123 and 69 units, respectively). Several key comparisons yield marginal p-values (p = 0.02 for Fisher's Exact Test on cell-type proportions; p = 0.04 for firing rate ratio distribution) without correction for the numerous comparisons performed throughout the paper. The population vector segmentation claim for cKO mice lacks any formal statistical test and relies on visual comparison of autocorrelation matrices, which is further weakened by the smaller sample producing less statistical power.

- **The ~10% firing shift from goal to delay activity is anecdotally reported (Supporting Claim on learning-related shifts):** The observation that ~10% of neurons (4/33) shift firing patterns after delay experience is presented without formal statistics, without verification that the same neurons are tracked across sessions, and the irreversibility claim is based on the same 4 neurons. This evidence is insufficient to support claims about experience-dependent plasticity of delay representations.

- **Binary classification of delay-act/sup populations may obscure a continuum:** The classification of 86.9% of neurons into two categories suggests the threshold may be overly liberal, or that essentially all CA1 neurons modulate firing during delay (possibly reflecting spatial or behavioral state effects rather than delay-specific coding). No bimodality test is reported. The authors should test whether the distribution of delay-zone firing rate modulation is bimodal or continuous.

**Minor Comments**

- The F-statistic notation in the cKO behavioral analysis is non-standard and potentially contains labeling errors (e.g., "F₁,₄ = 1.07, p = 0.37" for the delay main effect is unexpected given the clear delay-dependent effect visible in the data). The authors should verify and clarify all degrees of freedom.

- The spectrogram and SWR analyses (Figure 2) are based on very limited data (3 mice, 6 sessions for spectrograms; 1–3 sessions for SWRs). While these analyses serve as supporting context rather than core evidence, the limited sampling should be noted more prominently.

- The SVM decoding analysis should clarify how pseudo-populations were constructed and how the 0 s delay condition was handled (no actual delay period exists in this condition).

- The term "deformative normalization" is vague and non-standard; a more precise description of why mean firing rate normalization across different delay durations is problematic would improve clarity.

- The paper would benefit from reporting effect sizes alongside p-values throughout, particularly for the reward manipulation analyses where actual mean firing rate ratios are not provided.

- The Discussion's speculation linking NMDAR knockout phenotype to compulsive behavior (OCD) is interesting but should be more clearly flagged as speculative, given that it is not tested and multiple alternative interpretations are equally consistent with the data.

**Recommendation**

Major Revisions

The paper addresses an important question with a creative multi-condition experimental design, and several primary findings (population classification, SVM decoding, cKO behavioral phenotype) are well-supported. However, three critical issues must be resolved before the paper meets eLife's standards: (1) the peak firing rate confound in rate-coding analyses must be formally addressed or these claims withdrawn; (2) the value-coding and subjective value claims require formal statistical support or should be substantially downgraded to speculation; and (3) the NMDAR results require either histological verification of deletion specificity in recorded animals or substantial reframing of the interpretive claims. With 18 high-support but 7 low-support claims — several of which are central to the paper's narrative — the current evidence does not yet compellingly support the paper's most novel conceptual contributions.
