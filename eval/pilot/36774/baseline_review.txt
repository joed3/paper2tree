**eLife Assessment**
This valuable study provides solid evidence that neural activity in output-null dimensions of a brain-computer interface is coupled with output-potent activity, rather than being independently minimized or left uncontrolled. However, the mechanistic interpretation of this coupling and the generalizability beyond the specific BCI context rest on incomplete evidence that would benefit from additional analyses and discussion.

**Summary**
This paper investigates how the nervous system resolves neural redundancy — the fact that many different population activity patterns can produce the same behavioral output. Using a BCI paradigm in which three rhesus macaques controlled a 2D cursor via primary motor cortex (M1) activity projected through a known linear mapping, the authors systematically compare six hypotheses for how activity in the eight output-null dimensions is selected. The hypotheses span a spectrum from highly constrained (minimal firing) to largely unconstrained (minimal intervention/uncontrolled). By comparing activity across two different BCI mappings within each session, the authors find that a "Fixed Distribution" hypothesis — positing that the conditional distribution of output-null activity given output-potent activity is preserved across mappings — best predicts the observed data. This finding is interpreted as evidence that output-null and output-potent activity are coupled, which the authors argue constrains the extent to which neural circuits can exploit redundant dimensions for independent computation.

**Strengths**
- The BCI framework provides an exceptionally clean experimental setup for studying neural redundancy, as the mapping between neural activity and behavioral output is fully specified by the experimenter, eliminating the ambiguity inherent in studying redundancy in natural motor control.
- The systematic comparison of six hypotheses spanning a broad theoretical spectrum (minimal energy, minimal intervention, and coupled activity) is well-structured and provides strong inferential power. The hypotheses are well-motivated by existing literature on muscular redundancy.
- The use of internal model estimation (IME) to account for incomplete learning of the second BCI mapping is a thoughtful methodological choice that strengthens the conclusions, and the demonstration that results hold without IME adds robustness.
- The analysis of dimensions that changed from output-potent to output-null (Figure 6) is particularly compelling, as it tests hypotheses in exactly the dimensions where differences between predictions are most pronounced.
- Multiple complementary error metrics (histogram, mean, covariance) converge on the same conclusion, and the error floor analysis provides a useful benchmark for evaluating absolute prediction quality.
- The dataset is substantial (42 sessions across three animals), and results are consistent across animals.

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**
- **Asymmetry in hypothesis information content**: The Fixed Distribution hypothesis has access to the full empirical joint distribution of activity from the previous mapping, while competing hypotheses use substantially less empirical information (or none at all, in the case of Minimal Firing and Uncontrolled-uniform). This makes the comparison inherently asymmetric. The authors should explicitly quantify the "effective degrees of freedom" in each hypothesis and discuss whether the superior performance of Fixed Distribution partly reflects its richer parameterization rather than a deeper principle. A model comparison framework penalizing complexity (e.g., cross-validated likelihood) would strengthen the conclusions.

- **Mechanistic interpretation is underdeveloped**: The Fixed Distribution hypothesis is fundamentally descriptive — it states that the joint distribution is preserved but does not explain *why* the coupling exists. Is this coupling a consequence of fixed network connectivity in M1? Of shared inputs from upstream areas? Of the factor analysis model itself imposing covariance structure? The paper would benefit from a more thorough discussion of mechanistic explanations and, ideally, computational modeling to distinguish between them.

- **Potential confound from factor analysis**: The intrinsic manifold is identified via factor analysis, which inherently captures shared covariance structure. The observed coupling between output-potent and output-null dimensions could partly reflect the statistical structure imposed by the FA model rather than a biological constraint. The authors should analyze whether the degree of coupling depends on the FA model order (e.g., using fewer or more latent dimensions) or persists under alternative dimensionality reduction methods (e.g., PCA without the private variance separation).

- **Limited generalizability**: The paper's title and framing suggest broad conclusions about neural redundancy, but the results are specific to M1 activity during BCI control with a 2D output and within-manifold perturbations. The within-manifold perturbation design means both mappings respect the same intrinsic manifold, which could bias toward preserving joint distributions. The authors should more explicitly discuss how their conclusions might differ for outside-manifold perturbations, natural motor control with higher output dimensionality, or sensory/cognitive circuits.

- **Timescale concerns**: All analyses are conducted within single sessions (1–2 hours of the second mapping). The observed coupling may reflect the short timescale over which the neural population has had to reorganize, rather than a fundamental constraint. The authors acknowledge this possibility in the Discussion but should provide quantitative evidence — for example, does the prediction error of Fixed Distribution change as a function of time within the session, suggesting gradual decoupling?

- **Statistical rigor of distributional comparisons**: The histogram-based error metric, while intuitive, is sensitive to bin width and number. The authors should report results using more principled distributional distance measures (e.g., KL divergence, Wasserstein distance, or maximum mean discrepancy) to ensure robustness. Additionally, formal statistical tests comparing the prediction errors of Fixed Distribution versus each alternative should be reported with effect sizes, not just p-values.

**Minor Comments**
- Figure 1C–D is helpful for building intuition, but the simplified 2-neuron example may be misleading given the much higher dimensionality of the actual data. Consider adding a panel illustrating the 10D structure schematically.
- The paper states that the error floor is ~7% on average but Fixed Distribution achieves ~13%. The remaining ~6% gap is non-trivial and worth discussing — what might account for it?
- The Discussion section on OFC is somewhat speculative. The suggestion that OFC might work with PMd-to-M1 inputs as the control signal is interesting but could be developed more concretely or removed.
- The exclusion criteria for sessions (12 of 54 discarded) and time steps (distance-based exclusion) should be justified more thoroughly, with sensitivity analyses showing results are robust to these choices.
- The claim that arm movements were absent during BCI control (citing Extended Data Figure 5 of Sadtler et al., 2014) is critical for interpretation. The authors should provide more detail on how arm movement was monitored and quantified.

**Recommendation**
Major Revisions
The core finding is interesting and well-supported by the data, but the asymmetry in hypothesis testing, the lack of mechanistic explanation, potential confounds from the dimensionality reduction approach, and limited generalizability need to be addressed before the paper meets eLife's standards for a conceptual advance with convincing evidence.
