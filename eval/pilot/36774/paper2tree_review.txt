**eLife Assessment**

This study presents a valuable investigation into how neural redundancy is resolved during brain-computer interface control, providing solid evidence that the distribution of output-null neural activity is coupled to output-potent activity rather than governed by minimal energy or minimal intervention principles. However, the broader claims about constraints on neural computation remain inadequately supported by the experimental evidence, which is limited to a specific BCI paradigm over short timescales.

**Summary**

This paper asks how the brain resolves neural redundancy — the fact that many different patterns of population activity can produce the same behavioral output. Using a brain-computer interface (BCI) in which rhesus macaques controlled a cursor via primary motor cortex (M1) activity, the authors precisely defined which neural activity dimensions were output-potent (affecting the cursor) and which were output-null (redundant). By comparing two different BCI mappings that shared the same intrinsic manifold but differed in their output-potent subspaces, the authors tested six hypotheses about what governs activity in the output-null dimensions. The central empirical finding is that the "Fixed Distribution" hypothesis — which posits that the conditional distribution of output-null activity given output-potent activity is preserved across BCI mappings — best predicts the observed data in 41 of 42 sessions across three animals. The authors interpret this coupling between potent and null activity as evidence that neural redundancy is resolved differently from muscular redundancy and that this coupling may limit the brain's ability to exploit null-space activity for independent computation.

**Strengths**

- **Systematic hypothesis comparison (Primary Claim: Fixed Distribution best predicts output-null activity):** The paper compares six well-defined hypotheses with clearly distinct predictions using consistent error metrics. The Fixed Distribution hypothesis achieved the lowest histogram error in 41 of 42 sessions across three animals (Wilcoxon signed rank test, α = 0.001), providing compelling evidence that it outperforms all tested alternatives. The convergence across three error metrics (histogram, mean, covariance) further strengthens this conclusion.

- **Rejection of minimal energy principles (Primary Claim: Minimal firing/energy hypotheses fail):** The Minimal Firing hypothesis (73.2% error) and Minimal Deviation hypothesis (30.9% error) both performed far worse than the Fixed Distribution (13.4%), clearly ruling out firing-rate-based energy minimization as the dominant principle. The complementary variance analysis (Figure 6C) provides independent corroboration — observed variance decreases in potent→null dimensions were far smaller than predicted by minimal energy hypotheses.

- **Rejection of minimal intervention principle (Primary Claim: Uncontrolled manifold predictions fail):** Three implementations of the minimal intervention principle (Uncontrolled-uniform: 56.6%, Uncontrolled-empirical: 23.8%, Persistent Strategy: 17.4%) all performed worse than Fixed Distribution. The finding that variance did not increase in dimensions transitioning from potent to null directly contradicts the uncontrolled manifold hypothesis, a standard prediction in motor control.

- **Critical variance analysis (Supporting Claim: Variance does not change dramatically across potent/null transitions):** The analysis of variance in the 2D subspace that transitioned between potent and null designations is a particularly clean test, as it is where hypotheses make maximally different predictions. The small but significant decrease in the potent→null direction was itself predicted by the Fixed Distribution hypothesis, demonstrating nuanced quantitative agreement.

- **Robustness checks (Supporting Claim: Results robust to direction of analysis, internal model, individual animals):** Results hold when predicting the first mapping from the second (and vice versa), when using the actual BCI mapping rather than the estimated internal model, and for each animal individually. These controls substantially increase confidence in the core finding.

- **BCI as an experimental tool (Primary Claim: BCI allows precise definition of redundancy):** The use of a BCI with an experimenter-defined linear mapping provides ground-truth knowledge of output-potent and output-null dimensions — something unavailable in natural motor control. This is a genuine methodological strength for the specific question of redundancy resolution.

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**

- **Computational implications are unsupported (Primary Claim, low support: Coupling limits computation in null space):** The paper's most prominent interpretive claim — that coupling between potent and null activity "limits the extent to which redundancy may be exploited by the brain for computation" — is entirely speculative. No computation in the null space was measured, modeled, or attempted. The paper does not quantify what computations could or could not be performed given the observed coupling. This claim should be substantially hedged or removed from the abstract and framing, or the authors should provide a formal analysis (e.g., information-theoretic bounds) of how the observed coupling constrains specific computations.

- **Speculative extrapolation to other brain areas and functions (Supporting Claim, low support: Coupling may affect stimulus-null space or movement planning):** The suggestion that coupling could influence stimulus-null space computations or limit movement planning draws on work in entirely different brain areas (e.g., prefrontal cortex, Mante et al., 2013) and cognitive functions not tested here. The BCI task involves no stimulus discrimination and no multi-movement planning. These speculations should be clearly labeled as such and separated from the empirical findings.

- **Generalizability from BCI to natural neural circuits (Root Claim weakness; Required Assumption):** The root claim that coupling "constrains how neural circuits can exploit redundancy for computation" extrapolates from a highly artificial setting (10D intrinsic manifold, 2D cursor control, center-out reaching) to natural circuits with thousands of neurons and complex downstream targets. The paper itself acknowledges (Supporting Claim on short timescales) that with more practice "there may be different and perhaps fewer constraints," which undermines the generality of the main conclusion. The authors should more clearly delineate what the BCI results demonstrate versus what they hypothesize about natural circuits.

- **Comparison to muscular redundancy is limited (Primary Claim, medium support: Neural redundancy resolved differently from muscular redundancy):** The claim that neural and muscular redundancy are resolved "differently" compares neural activity in a BCI task to muscular activity during natural movement — fundamentally different systems. Moreover, the authors themselves note that habitual control (de Rugy et al., 2012) shows coupling of task-relevant and task-irrelevant activity in muscles, partially contradicting the claim that the principles differ. The authors also acknowledge that OFC with alternative cost functions could potentially explain the coupling, further weakening this claim. A more measured framing would acknowledge these parallels explicitly.

- **"Exceedingly close" to floor is overstated (Supporting Claim, medium support):** The characterization of Fixed Distribution errors as "exceedingly close" to the error floor requires revision. The histogram error (13.4%) is roughly double the floor (6.7%); the mean error (23.5 spikes/s) is 3.4× the floor (6.9 spikes/s). These gaps are non-trivial and could reflect systematic discrepancies not captured by any tested hypothesis. The authors should either provide a formal statistical argument for closeness or use more measured language.

- **Hypothesis space is not exhaustive (Primary Claim weakness):** The six hypotheses tested, while well-chosen, do not span all plausible mechanisms. Models based on recurrent network dynamics, nonlinear coupling, or OFC with non-standard cost functions were not tested. The paper should acknowledge more explicitly that the Fixed Distribution hypothesis may succeed because it captures the intrinsic manifold structure rather than because of a deep principle about redundancy resolution.

- **Arm movement confound not empirically ruled out (Supporting Claim weakness):** While the authors argue that the arm was not moving during BCI control, no empirical verification (e.g., EMG recordings, motion tracking) is described. The claim that BCI and arm movement dimensions do not overlap is asserted without proof. Subthreshold muscle activation or motor imagery could create coupling patterns in M1 activity. The authors should describe how arm immobility was verified or acknowledge this limitation.

**Minor Comments**

- The one session (of 42) where Fixed Distribution was not the best hypothesis is not discussed. Understanding what made this session different could be informative about boundary conditions of the finding.

- Exact p-values should be reported rather than only significance thresholds (α = 0.001), particularly for the primary hypothesis comparisons.

- The covariance error is reported in "arbitrary units," making practical significance difficult to assess. A normalized covariance metric or explanation of scale would improve interpretability.

- The number of sessions per animal should be clearly stated in the main text, not only in supplementary materials.

- The supplemental analyses (individual animals, reverse prediction direction, actual vs. estimated mapping) are important for the paper's conclusions but are not quantitatively described in the main text. Key numerical results from these analyses should be reported.

- The analysis of variance changes is limited to a 2D subspace of the 8D output-null space. The authors should discuss whether this subspace is representative or whether the pattern holds more broadly.

**Recommendation**

Major Revisions

The core empirical finding — that the Fixed Distribution hypothesis best predicts output-null activity — is supported by compelling evidence across 39 high-support claims. However, the two low-support claims concern the paper's central interpretive message (that coupling constrains neural computation), which is prominently featured in the abstract and discussion but lacks empirical grounding. The comparison to muscular redundancy and the characterization of proximity to the error floor also require revision. The paper would benefit substantially from (1) tempering the computational implications claims, (2) more carefully distinguishing empirical findings from speculation, and (3) addressing the generalizability limitations of the BCI paradigm.
