**eLife Assessment**
This valuable study presents solid evidence that postsynaptic RA/RARα signaling regulates BDNF synthesis to drive retrograde modulation of presynaptic function during homeostatic plasticity, though several central mechanistic claims rest on incomplete evidence and would benefit from additional experiments demonstrating in vivo RARα–Bdnf mRNA interactions, translation-dependence of BDNF increases, and direct functional coupling between RA-induced BDNF release and presynaptic changes.

**Summary**
The study investigates how postsynaptic retinoic acid (RA) signaling modulates presynaptic function during homeostatic synaptic plasticity at hippocampal excitatory synapses. Using organotypic hippocampal slice cultures from conditional knockout mice (RARα, BDNF, TrkB) combined with region-specific AAV-Cre injections in CA3 versus CA1, the authors demonstrate that: (1) postsynaptic, but not presynaptic, RARα is required for RA- or CNQX-induced increases in both mEPSC amplitude and frequency; (2) RARα binds dendritically localized Bdnf mRNA isoforms (containing exon 2 and exon 6) in vitro; (3) RA stimulates proBDNF synthesis in hippocampal synaptoneurosomes; and (4) postsynaptic BDNF and presynaptic TrkB are each required for the homeostatic increase in mEPSC frequency but not amplitude. Collectively, these results are used to construct a model in which chronic synaptic inactivity triggers postsynaptic RA synthesis, RARα-mediated translational de-repression of dendritic Bdnf mRNAs, and retrograde BDNF signaling through presynaptic TrkB to enhance neurotransmitter release probability.

**Strengths**
- The experimental strategy of using region-specific (CA3 vs. CA1) AAV-Cre injections in conditional knockout mice to separately delete RARα, BDNF, or TrkB pre- versus postsynaptically is elegant and provides strong genetic evidence for the loci of action of each signaling molecule.
- The consistent replication of key findings across two induction paradigms (acute RA and chronic CNQX) strengthens the physiological relevance and internal validity of the study.
- The RNA-binding assay includes well-designed negative controls (GST alone, RARα LBD ΔF, RARβ LBD, and non-binding dendritic mRNAs such as Psd95 and Camk2a), lending credibility to the specificity of RARα–Bdnf mRNA interactions.
- The dissociation between BDNF/TrkB signaling (exclusively affecting frequency) and the postsynaptic AMPAR insertion pathway (affecting amplitude) provides a clean separation of pre- and postsynaptic homeostatic mechanisms.
- The paired-pulse ratio and minimal stimulation experiments (Figure 1—figure supplement 1) appropriately address alternative explanations (silent synapse unsilencing) for the frequency increase.

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**
- **Lack of in vivo/in situ validation of RARα–Bdnf mRNA binding.** The RNA-binding data rely entirely on an in vitro GST-pulldown assay using recombinant protein and bulk hippocampal RNA. RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) or CLIP from neuronal lysates, or FISH combined with RARα immunostaining demonstrating co-localization in dendrites, would substantially strengthen the claim that this interaction is physiologically relevant.
- **No evidence that the proBDNF increase in synaptoneurosomes reflects de novo translation.** The synaptoneurosome experiment (Figure 2E) shows increased proBDNF after RA treatment but lacks a protein synthesis inhibitor control (e.g., anisomycin or cycloheximide). Without this, the increase could reflect reduced degradation or altered processing rather than new translation. This is a critical gap given that translational de-repression is the proposed mechanism.
- **Absence of rescue or direct functional coupling experiments.** The model would be considerably strengthened by showing that (a) exogenous BDNF rescues the mEPSC frequency deficit caused by postsynaptic BDNF deletion, or (b) BDNF scavenging (e.g., TrkB-Fc) blocks RA-induced frequency increases, or (c) a TrkB agonist rescues the frequency deficit in presynaptic TrkB KO conditions. Currently, the genetic loss-of-function experiments are necessary but not sufficient to establish a direct causal chain.
- **No evidence of BDNF secretion from postsynaptic dendrites.** The retrograde signaling model requires that newly synthesized BDNF is released from dendrites. No secretion assay (e.g., BDNF-pHluorin imaging, ELISA from conditioned media) is provided. This is particularly important because BDNF release is typically considered activity-dependent, and the context here is chronic inactivity.
- **Statistical concerns with interaction terms.** In Figure 3C (frequency), the two-way ANOVA interaction term is non-significant (F(2,195) = 2.53, p > 0.05), yet post-hoc pairwise comparisons are performed and interpreted as showing differential effects of CNQX across genotypes. Similarly, in Figure 3F (frequency), the interaction term is also non-significant (F(2,168) = 0.17, p > 0.8). These non-significant interactions undermine the claim that the genotype specifically modulates the CNQX effect on frequency. The authors should either use a statistical framework appropriate for these data or temper their conclusions accordingly.
- **No biochemical verification of knockout efficiency.** While AAV infection rates are shown to be >90% by fluorescence (Figure 1—figure supplement 1C), no immunoblotting or immunostaining data confirm actual reduction in RARα, BDNF, or TrkB protein levels in the targeted regions. This is especially important for BDNF, whose protein has a long half-life and may persist after gene deletion.
- **The specificity of Bdnf transcript involvement is not functionally tested.** While the binding data implicate exon 2 and exon 6 transcripts, no isoform-specific manipulation (e.g., isoform-selective knockdown, or mutation of the RARα-binding motif in the 5'UTR) is performed to demonstrate that these specific transcripts are the functionally relevant ones.
- **Temporal considerations are not addressed.** The 4-hour RA treatment must be sufficient for translation, post-translational processing, and secretion of BDNF, followed by presynaptic TrkB activation and modulation of release machinery. Is this timeline plausible? Time-course experiments or use of protein synthesis inhibitors applied at different intervals could help clarify this.

**Minor Comments**
- The paper appears to present only three main figures plus supplements, which feels sparse for a full research article at eLife. Additional supporting data (e.g., BDNF immunostaining in slices after RA/CNQX treatment, verification of Cre-mediated deletion) would strengthen the manuscript.
- The mIPSC data (Figure 1—figure supplement 1D) showing RA-dependent reductions in both amplitude and frequency at inhibitory synapses in older cultures is interesting and relevant but is essentially dropped without investigation of underlying mechanisms. The authors should either expand on this or discuss why it was excluded from further study more explicitly.
- The discussion of the relationship between RA/RARα and mTORC1 pathways is speculative and could be more clearly delineated as an open question versus an established connection.
- Figure panel labeling and cross-referencing in the text could be improved; the manuscript text intermingles figure legends with narrative in places, making it difficult to follow the experimental flow.
- The choice to use CD1 mice for biochemistry but conditional knockout lines (presumably on C57BL/6 background) for electrophysiology should be noted and justified, as strain differences in BDNF expression and synaptic plasticity have been documented.
- The PPR data (Figure 1—figure supplement 1A) would benefit from reporting exact ISI values and sample sizes in the figure or legend.
- GFP-only AAV controls (without Cre) should be included to rule out non-specific effects of viral infection on synaptic transmission.

**Recommendation**
Major Revisions
The study addresses an important mechanistic question and employs a strong genetic strategy, but key links in the proposed causal chain—particularly in vivo RARα–Bdnf mRNA interaction, translation-dependence of the BDNF increase, functional rescue experiments, and statistical validity of several central comparisons—require additional experimental evidence before the conclusions can be considered fully supported at eLife's standards.
