**eLife Assessment**

This study presents a **valuable** model linking RA/RARα-dependent dendritic BDNF synthesis to retrograde presynaptic homeostatic plasticity via TrkB signaling. However, the evidence is **incomplete**: while the conditional knockout experiments with acute RA treatment provide solid support for compartment-specific roles of RARα, BDNF, and TrkB, critical statistical failures in the chronic CNQX experiments—the more physiologically relevant condition—substantially weaken the central claims, and the mechanistic chain from RARα-mRNA binding to functional translational de-repression lacks direct causal demonstration.

**Summary**

This paper proposes that during prolonged synaptic inactivity, retinoic acid (RA) acts through postsynaptic RARα to de-repress translation of dendritically localized *Bdnf* mRNA isoforms (containing exon 2 and exon 6), leading to local BDNF synthesis and retrograde activation of presynaptic TrkB receptors to enhance neurotransmitter release. The authors employ region-specific AAV-Cre delivery in conditional knockout mice for RARα, BDNF, and TrkB in organotypic hippocampal slices, enabling selective deletion in presynaptic (CA3) versus postsynaptic (CA1) compartments. They combine this genetic dissection with in vitro RNA-binding assays, synaptoneurosome translation experiments, in silico motif analysis, and developmental expression profiling to build a multi-step mechanistic model. The study extends prior work on RA-mediated homeostatic plasticity by identifying BDNF as a specific translational target of RARα and proposing a complete retrograde signaling pathway from postsynaptic RA synthesis to presynaptic functional compensation.

**Strengths**

- **Postsynaptic RARα requirement (high support):** The conditional knockout strategy with region-specific AAV-Cre delivery provides clean genetic evidence that postsynaptic—but not presynaptic—RARα is required for RA-induced increases in both mEPSC amplitude and frequency. The significant two-way ANOVA interaction effects (amplitude p<0.01; frequency p<0.0001 with RA; both p<0.01 with CNQX) and replication across two induction methods (acute RA and chronic CNQX) make this one of the strongest findings in the paper.

- **TrkB compartment specificity with RA (high support):** The presynaptic TrkB KO experiment with acute RA treatment yields a highly significant frequency interaction (F(2,147)=14.87, p<0.0001) with no effect on amplitude (p>0.9), while postsynaptic TrkB KO has no effect. This clean dissociation strongly supports the retrograde signaling model under pharmacological RA conditions.

- **BDNF/TrkB selectivity for presynaptic function (high support):** Across all BDNF and TrkB KO conditions, amplitude changes are consistently preserved (all interaction p-values non-significant), while frequency changes are selectively affected. This consistent pattern across multiple experiments and gene deletions supports the mechanistic specificity of BDNF-TrkB signaling for presynaptic modulation.

- **Developmental BDNF expression profiling (high support):** The characterization of proBDNF upregulation across five in vivo timepoints and four in vitro timepoints is well-executed, with appropriate statistical analyses, and demonstrates that the organotypic culture system recapitulates the in vivo developmental trajectory.

- **Synaptoneurosome specificity controls (high support):** The GluA1 increase (p<0.005) and PSD95 non-response in RA-treated synaptoneurosomes provide internally consistent positive and negative controls that align with the RNA-binding data and prior literature (Aoto et al., 2008).

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**

- **Non-significant CNQX interactions for BDNF and TrkB KO (low support):** This is the most critical concern. The CNQX experiment with BDNF KO yields a non-significant frequency interaction (F(2,195)=2.53, p>0.05; Figure 3C), and the TrkB KO CNQX experiment is even weaker (F(2,168)=0.17, p>0.8; Figure 3F). Despite these non-significant interactions, the paper claims that postsynaptic BDNF deletion and presynaptic TrkB deletion block CNQX-induced frequency changes, apparently relying on post-hoc tests. This is statistically inappropriate and fundamentally undermines the generalizability of the pathway to the more physiologically relevant condition of chronic activity blockade. The authors must either provide adequately powered replication of these experiments, acknowledge the discrepancy transparently, or reframe their conclusions to apply only to acute RA treatment.

- **Absence of causal link between RARα-mRNA binding and translational regulation (medium-low support):** The RNA-binding assay (Figure 2D) and the synaptoneurosome translation assay (Figure 2E) are presented as mechanistically connected, but no experiment directly tests whether RARα binding to *Bdnf* exon 2/6 mRNAs is required for the RA-induced BDNF protein increase. Essential missing experiments include: (1) RARα-KO synaptoneurosomes to confirm RA-induced BDNF synthesis is RARα-dependent; (2) mutation of the putative RARα-binding motifs in *Bdnf* exon 2/6 to test whether binding is necessary for translational regulation; or (3) CLIP-seq/RIP in neurons to validate binding in a cellular context.

- **In vitro RNA-binding assay limitations (medium support):** The RNA pulldown uses purified GST fusion proteins with denatured/renatured total hippocampal RNA, and the readout is semi-quantitative gel-based RT-PCR without band quantification or statistical analysis across the three replicates. No in vivo or cellular validation (e.g., CLIP-seq, RNA immunoprecipitation from neurons) is provided. Given that this assay underpins the claim that RARα "directly binds" specific *Bdnf* isoforms, the authors should provide quantitative analysis across replicates and ideally cellular validation of the interaction.

- **Marginal proBDNF increase in synaptoneurosomes (medium support):** The RA-induced proBDNF increase reaches only p<0.05 significance (Figure 2E), which is marginal for a central claim. No translation inhibitor control (cycloheximide/anisomycin) distinguishes new synthesis from reduced degradation. Additionally, there appears to be a discrepancy between the text (30-minute RA treatment) and methods (10-minute treatment at 37°C) that must be resolved.

- **Developmental correlation claim (low support):** The claim that BDNF availability underlies the age-dependent emergence of presynaptic homeostatic plasticity is entirely correlative. No experiment tests whether premature BDNF expression enables presynaptic homeostatic plasticity in younger cultures or whether BDNF depletion specifically prevents it at the developmental transition. The authors should either provide causal evidence or substantially moderate this claim.

- **No demonstration of BDNF secretion or retrograde delivery:** The model requires that RA-induced proBDNF (or processed mature BDNF) is secreted from postsynaptic compartments and reaches presynaptic terminals. No experiment addresses BDNF secretion, processing, or retrograde delivery. This gap should be acknowledged as a limitation.

- **Baseline synaptic properties not reported:** For all conditional KO experiments, baseline mEPSC properties (amplitude and frequency in the control/vehicle condition) are not compared across genotypes. Given that AAV-Cre is delivered at DIV0, developmental effects of gene deletion on basal synaptic transmission cannot be excluded and could confound the interpretation of homeostatic plasticity experiments.

**Minor Comments**

- The in silico analysis of RARα-binding motifs (Figure 2—figure supplement 1B) does not describe the algorithm or criteria used, making reproducibility difficult. The computational methods should be described in sufficient detail.

- The paper uses "unequivocally" in the abstract to describe the establishment of the retrograde signaling pathway. Given the statistical weaknesses in the CNQX experiments and the absence of direct causal links between binding and translation, this language should be moderated.

- Post-hoc tests throughout the paper use Mann-Whitney U tests without explicit correction for multiple comparisons. Given the 3×2 ANOVA design (three genotypes × two treatments), appropriate corrections (e.g., Bonferroni, Tukey's) should be applied.

- The claim regarding conservation of RA effects on inhibitory transmission across brain regions (comparison to visual cortex, Zhong et al., 2018) is speculative and based on very indirect parallels. This should be more clearly flagged as speculation.

- PPR data and silent synapse analysis are relegated to supplementary figures but are important for the central claim that frequency changes reflect presynaptic release probability changes. Consider elevating these to main figures.

- The argument that new synapse formation does not underlie frequency changes relies solely on the logic that older cultures show less synaptogenesis; direct measurement (e.g., spine density, synapse counts) would strengthen this claim.

**Recommendation**

Major Revisions

The conditional knockout experiments with acute RA treatment provide solid evidence for compartment-specific roles of RARα, BDNF, and TrkB, but the non-significant ANOVA interaction terms in the CNQX experiments (the physiologically relevant condition) for both BDNF and TrkB deletions represent a critical gap that must be addressed through either adequately powered replication or transparent reframing of the conclusions. Additionally, the mechanistic chain from RARα-mRNA binding to translational de-repression requires at least one experiment demonstrating RARα-dependence of RA-induced BDNF synthesis (e.g., RARα-KO synaptoneurosomes) to meet eLife's standards for a mechanistic claim.
