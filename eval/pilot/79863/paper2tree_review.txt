**eLife Assessment**

This valuable study proposes a mechanistically coherent model linking postsynaptic RA/RARα signaling to presynaptic homeostatic plasticity through retrograde BDNF-TrkB signaling, but the central claim rests on incomplete evidence: critical statistical inconsistencies in the CNQX replication experiments, the absence of in vivo validation for the RNA-binding data, and the lack of direct demonstration that RA-induced BDNF synthesis requires RARα weaken the overall conclusions.

**Summary**

Thapliyal et al. investigate how postsynaptic retinoic acid (RA) signaling drives presynaptic homeostatic changes at hippocampal excitatory synapses. Using organotypic hippocampal slice cultures from conditional knockout mice for RARα, BDNF, and TrkB, combined with region-specific AAV-Cre delivery to achieve pre- versus postsynaptic gene deletion at Schaffer collateral–CA1 synapses, the authors report that: (1) postsynaptic but not presynaptic RARα is required for RA-mediated increases in both mEPSC amplitude and frequency; (2) RARα directly binds dendritically localized *Bdnf* mRNA isoforms (exon 2 and exon 6) via its F domain in vitro; (3) RA stimulates proBDNF protein synthesis in hippocampal synaptoneurosomes; and (4) postsynaptic BDNF and presynaptic TrkB are specifically required for the homeostatic increase in mEPSC frequency but not amplitude. The paper additionally documents a temporal correlation between developmental BDNF upregulation and the maturation-dependent emergence of presynaptic homeostatic plasticity. The proposed model—that chronic synaptic inactivity triggers dendritic RA synthesis, which de-represses RARα-bound *Bdnf* mRNAs, leading to retrograde BDNF-TrkB signaling that enhances presynaptic release—would represent a significant conceptual advance in understanding how postsynaptic signaling coordinates presynaptic homeostatic compensation at mammalian central synapses.

**Strengths**

- The experimental design using region-specific AAV-Cre in conditional knockout mice for three different genes (RARα, BDNF, TrkB) to dissect pre- versus postsynaptic loci of action is elegant and well-controlled. The postsynaptic RARα requirement is strongly supported, with significant two-way ANOVA interaction effects for both RA treatment (amplitude F(2,208)=5.413, p<0.01; frequency F(2,208)=11.81, p<0.0001) and CNQX treatment (amplitude F(2,135)=6.004, p<0.01; frequency F(2,135)=5.23, p<0.01).

- The double dissociation between BDNF (postsynaptic requirement) and TrkB (presynaptic requirement) for the frequency component, with neither affecting the amplitude component, is a compelling result in the RA treatment experiments (TrkB frequency interaction: F(2,147)=14.87, p<0.0001).

- The convergence of three electrophysiological measures—increased mEPSC frequency, reduced paired-pulse ratio, and absence of silent synapse unmasking—to support enhanced presynaptic release provides internally consistent evidence for the presynaptic locus of the frequency change.

- The in vitro RNA-binding assay includes multiple well-chosen controls: GST alone, RARα LBD ΔF, RARβ LBD, the positive control Gria1, negative controls (Psd95, Camk2a, Ef1a), and a within-family negative control (Bdnf exon 1, which lacks dendritic targeting and carries only one predicted motif).

- The developmental BDNF expression profile is documented both in vivo (P1–P28) and in the organotypic slice system (1–21 DIV), demonstrating that the culture system preserves the in vivo trajectory.

- The replication of findings using both exogenous RA and endogenous RA synthesis (via CNQX) across multiple genetic conditions strengthens the internal validity of the RARα findings.

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**

- **Critical statistical inconsistencies in CNQX replication experiments undermine key claims.** For the BDNF KO + CNQX experiment (Figure 3C), the two-way ANOVA interaction for frequency is non-significant (F(2,195)=2.53, p>0.05), yet the authors report that postsynaptic BDNF deletion blocks the CNQX-induced frequency increase based on post-hoc Mann-Whitney tests. This is statistically inappropriate—post-hoc pairwise comparisons are not generally valid when the omnibus interaction test fails. Even more strikingly, the TrkB KO + CNQX experiment (Figure 3F) shows a grossly non-significant frequency interaction (F(2,168)=0.17, p>0.8), yet the authors again claim that presynaptic TrkB deletion blocks CNQX-induced frequency changes. This is in stark contrast to the highly significant RA result (p<0.0001) and represents a fundamental failure to replicate the central claim under the more physiologically relevant CNQX paradigm. The authors must address this discrepancy transparently and either provide additional data or temper their conclusions regarding BDNF-TrkB involvement in CNQX-induced presynaptic homeostatic plasticity.

- **The RNA-binding data lacks in vivo or cellular validation.** The claim that RARα directly binds *Bdnf* exon 2 and exon 6 mRNAs relies entirely on an in vitro GST pulldown assay using purified fusion proteins and total hippocampal RNA that was heat-denatured and renatured. This assay does not demonstrate that this interaction occurs in living neurons, in dendrites, or under physiological conditions. CLIP-seq, RNA immunoprecipitation from neurons, or at minimum co-localization of RARα protein with *Bdnf* mRNAs in dendrites would substantially strengthen this claim. Furthermore, the RT-PCR readout is semi-quantitative (gel-based) without band intensity quantification or statistical analysis across replicates.

- **The synaptoneurosome BDNF translation experiment does not demonstrate RARα-dependence.** While RA treatment increases proBDNF levels in synaptoneurosomes (p<0.05, which is marginal for a central finding), no experiment tests whether this increase requires RARα (e.g., using RARα-KO synaptoneurosomes or an RARα antagonist). This is a critical gap because the entire model posits that RA acts through RARα to de-repress *Bdnf* mRNA translation. Additionally, there is a potential discrepancy between the main text (stating 30-minute treatment) and the methods section (describing 10-minute treatment) that must be clarified. The authors should also test whether protein synthesis inhibitors (e.g., cycloheximide or anisomycin) block the proBDNF increase to rule out reduced degradation as an alternative explanation.

- **No direct evidence that RA-induced BDNF is secreted from dendrites or reaches presynaptic terminals.** The model requires that newly synthesized BDNF is released from postsynaptic compartments and activates presynaptic TrkB. However, no experiment demonstrates dendritic BDNF secretion following RA treatment. Furthermore, whether the relevant species is proBDNF or mature BDNF is not addressed—the synaptoneurosome experiment only measures proBDNF, and the functional form activating TrkB is not established.

- **The developmental correlation between BDNF expression and emergence of presynaptic homeostatic plasticity (Figure 2A, Figure 2—figure supplement 1A) is entirely correlative.** No causal experiment tests whether BDNF availability is the rate-limiting factor. Numerous other factors change between 14 and 21 DIV (synapse maturation, receptor composition, dendritic mRNA trafficking). Overexpression of BDNF in younger cultures to test whether it is sufficient to enable presynaptic homeostatic plasticity, or conversely, BDNF knockdown at 21 DIV to test necessity at that specific timepoint, would be informative.

- **Baseline synaptic properties in KO conditions are not reported.** For all conditional KO experiments (RARα, BDNF, TrkB), the paper does not compare baseline mEPSC properties (DMSO-treated) across genotypes. Given that AAV-Cre is injected at DIV0, deletion of these genes throughout the culture period could alter circuit development, synapse formation, or basal synaptic properties in ways that confound the interpretation of homeostatic plasticity experiments. Explicit comparison of baseline values across genotypes is essential.

- **The argument that new synapse formation does not underlie frequency increases is unsupported by direct evidence.** The claim relies on logical inference (frequency increase occurs in older cultures where synaptogenesis is "less robust") but provides no direct measurement of synapse numbers (e.g., spine density imaging, synaptophysin/PSD95 puncta quantification, or electron microscopy).

**Minor Comments**

- The in silico analysis of RARα-binding motifs in *Bdnf* exons is insufficiently described. The algorithm, parameters, and criteria used to define "RARα recognition motifs" should be specified, along with the false positive rate of the motif search.

- The 10 μM RA concentration used for acute treatment is supraphysiological. While the CNQX experiments partially address this concern by triggering endogenous RA synthesis, the authors should discuss whether this high concentration could produce off-target effects.

- The mIPSC data (Figure 1—figure supplement 1D) showing reduced frequency with RA treatment in older cultures is an interesting novel finding but is only tested with postsynaptic RARα KO. A presynaptic KO control would help distinguish whether the frequency change at inhibitory synapses also involves a trans-synaptic mechanism.

- The use of Mann-Whitney post-hoc tests following two-way ANOVA, rather than tests that correct for multiple comparisons (e.g., Tukey's, which is used for mIPSC data), should be justified or corrected for consistency across the manuscript.

- Figure quality: representative traces and gel images are clear, but quantification of RT-PCR band intensities across all three replicates would significantly strengthen Figure 2D.

- The methods state synaptoneurosomes were treated with RA for 10 minutes, but the main text and figure legend state 30 minutes. This discrepancy must be resolved.

**Recommendation**

Major Revisions

While the study addresses an important question with an elegant genetic approach and 23 of 50 claims are well-supported, the critical statistical failures in the CNQX replication experiments for BDNF and TrkB (two of five low-support claims directly undermining the central thesis), the absence of RARα-dependence testing for RA-induced BDNF translation, and the lack of in vivo validation for the RNA-binding interaction represent substantial gaps that must be addressed before the paper meets eLife's standards for convincing evidence supporting its mechanistic claims.
