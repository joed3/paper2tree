**eLife Assessment**

This study presents a valuable contribution to our understanding of metabolic flexibility in mammalian brain evolution, providing convincing genomic evidence for independent HMGCS2 gene loss in three mammalian lineages, though the broader interpretive claims about the relationship between ketogenesis and brain size evolution rest on incomplete metabolic evidence and temporal inferences with substantial uncertainty.

**Summary**

This paper investigates the evolution of HMGCS2, the gene encoding the rate-limiting enzyme for ketone body biosynthesis, across 70 placental mammals. The authors report the unexpected finding that HMGCS2 has been independently lost in three lineages — cetaceans, pteropodid bats, and Elephantimorpha (elephants and mastodons) — all of which possess relatively large brains. Using molecular dating, they argue that gene loss preceded brain size expansion in cetaceans and elephants, leading to the central thesis that ketogenesis is not a universal precondition for the evolution of large mammalian brains. The study further examines downstream pathway genes, finding that the non-pleiotropic gene BDH1 shows concordant relaxed selection in two of three lineages, while pleiotropic genes (ACAT1, HMGCL) remain intact. The authors conclude that alternative metabolic strategies to fuel large brains during fasting must have evolved independently at least twice.

The computational and genomic approach is rigorous, combining genome-wide screening, unassembled read validation, synteny analysis, RNA-seq interrogation, and molecular evolutionary analyses. However, the paper's broader claims about metabolic flexibility and the decoupling of ketogenesis from brain evolution extend substantially beyond what the genomic data alone can support, as no direct metabolic measurements are provided for any of the three lineages.

**Strengths**

- **HMGCS2 gene loss documentation is exceptionally thorough.** The convergence of multiple lines of evidence — large exon deletions, frameshift mutations, splice site disruptions, and stop codons — validated by unassembled sequencing reads (≥30 reads per mutation in cetaceans with no support for ancestral alleles), PacBio assembly confirmation in Egyptian fruit bat, and inclusion of ancient DNA from mastodons makes the core genomic finding highly robust.

- **Synteny-based orthology confirmation** across all three lineages (REG4 upstream and PHGDH downstream in cetaceans and pteropodids; PHGDH adjacency confirmed in elephants despite rearrangements) rules out misidentification of paralogs, a critical control for gene loss studies.

- **Strong purifying selection on HMGCS2 in 59 other mammals** (dN/dS = 0.16) effectively highlights how remarkable these three independent losses are, particularly given the severe clinical consequences of HMGCS2 deficiency in humans.

- **The pathway-level analysis of pleiotropic vs. non-pleiotropic gene loss** is elegant: BDH1 shows inactivating mutations and relaxed selection in cetaceans and pteropodids, while ACAT1 and HMGCL (with roles in amino acid metabolism) remain intact. This is consistent with well-established evolutionary principles and provides additional support for the biological reality of ketogenesis loss.

- **Shared inactivating mutations in cetaceans** (exon 1 deletion breakpoints, 2 bp insertion in exon 2 shared between toothed and baleen whales) provide unambiguous evidence that HMGCS2 was lost before the toothed/baleen whale divergence (~36 Mya), and the honest acknowledgment that the temporal ordering does not hold for pteropodids strengthens the paper's credibility.

- **The HMGCS1 compensation analysis** — combining TargetP predictions of mitochondrial targeting signals, Augustus gene predictions with alternative first exons, and liver RNA-seq from minke whale and Egyptian fruit bat — provides a multi-pronged argument against paralog rescue, though with caveats noted below.

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**

- **The claim that HMGCS2 loss results in "complete loss of ketogenesis" (Primary Claim 2) is an inference, not a demonstration.** No direct metabolic measurements — ketone body levels, metabolomic profiling, or enzyme activity assays — are provided for any species in any of the three lineages. The only metabolic data cited is from Ridgway (2013) for bottlenose dolphins. TargetP has known false-negative rates, and non-canonical mitochondrial import mechanisms could theoretically redirect HMGCS1. The authors should explicitly frame this as a strong prediction rather than an established fact, and discuss what experimental validation would look like.

- **The claim that species in all three lineages have "relatively large brains" demonstrating ketone bodies are not required for large brain fueling (Primary Claim 4) lacks statistical rigor.** No formal test is performed to assess whether HMGCS2-loss lineages have significantly larger brains than expected. Pteropodid EQ values, while >1, are modest compared to dolphins (EQ 3.7) and elephants. The paper should at minimum acknowledge that three lineages provide limited statistical power and that the association could be coincidental, given that all three lineages have other distinctive features (aquatic lifestyle, flight, large body size) that could independently drive both metabolic changes and brain evolution.

- **The temporal ordering argument for elephants (Primary Claim 6) has substantial uncertainty that is insufficiently acknowledged.** The gap between estimated gene loss (45-42 Mya) and brain expansion onset (~37 Mya) is only ~5-8 My, which falls within the combined uncertainty of molecular dating and fossil-based EQ estimation. The authors themselves note that "exact EQ values of Palaeomastodons are not known." The 70% synonymous rate assumption underlying the molecular dating should be discussed more critically, and sensitivity analyses with alternative rate assumptions would strengthen the claim.

- **The claim that "alternative metabolic strategies to fuel large brains during fasting evolved at least twice independently" (Primary Claim 7) is asserted but not demonstrated.** This paper identifies no alternative strategy for any lineage. For elephants, no data on fasting metabolism is presented; the blood pH observation during musth (the only LOW support claim in the entire paper, rated as such because blood alkalinity during musth could reflect hyperventilation, hormonal changes, or altered kidney function rather than absence of ketone bodies) is far too indirect. For pteropodids, the evidence actually suggests they *lack* an effective alternative (death after 24 hr fasting), which undermines this claim. The authors should more carefully distinguish between "absence of ketogenesis" and "presence of an alternative strategy."

- **The RNA-seq analysis for HMGCS1 alternative exons (Supporting Claim) has important limitations that should be discussed.** Data were available for only two of three lineages (no elephant), conditions under which samples were collected (fed vs. fasted) are not specified, and the sensitivity to detect low-abundance condition-specific transcripts is unknown. Fasting-induced alternative splicing producing an mTP-containing HMGCS1 transcript cannot be excluded.

- **BDH1 retention in elephants breaks the expected pattern** and requires explanation. If HMGCS2 loss eliminates ketogenesis, BDH1 should be under relaxed selection in elephants as well. The retention could indicate unknown pleiotropic functions of BDH1 or, more problematically, residual ketone body metabolism in elephants. The authors should discuss this asymmetry more thoroughly.

- **The root claim bundles two assertions with different levels of support.** The genomic evidence for HMGCS2 loss is compelling; the claim about "unexpected flexibility in mammalian energy metabolism" requires metabolic data that is not provided. The authors should consider whether the title claim — "ketogenesis is not essential for the evolution of large mammalian brains" — could be stated more precisely to reflect that this is inferred from gene loss rather than demonstrated metabolically.

**Minor Comments**

- The two species excluded due to assembly gaps (proboscis monkey and lesser Egyptian jerboa) should be noted as a limitation of the survey, even though they are unlikely to affect conclusions.

- The discussion of cognitive abilities (vocal learning, tool use, self-recognition) in HMGCS2-loss lineages, while interesting, is tangential to the metabolic argument and risks implying a connection between intelligence and metabolic fuel type that is not established.

- The conservation implication for pteropodid bats is speculative and would benefit from discussion of whether wild populations actually experience fasting durations sufficient to make ketogenesis relevant.

- Standard errors or confidence intervals for the dN/dS estimate of 0.16 and for the molecular dating estimates should be provided in the main text, not only in supplementary materials.

- The shrew BDH1 loss observation is interesting but receives limited analysis; it could be developed further or flagged as a direction for future work.

**Recommendation**

Minor Revisions

The core genomic finding — three independent HMGCS2 losses validated with multiple lines of evidence — is compelling (35 of 47 claims rated high support, only 1 low support), and represents a genuinely interesting observation for the field. However, several interpretive claims, particularly regarding complete loss of ketogenesis, the existence of alternative metabolic strategies, and the precise temporal ordering in elephants, require more careful framing, additional caveats, or toned-down language to accurately reflect the strength of available evidence before meeting eLife's standards for a paper making broad claims about mammalian brain evolution.
