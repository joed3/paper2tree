**eLife Assessment**

This valuable study provides convincing genomic evidence for the independent loss of the ketogenesis enzyme gene HMGCS2 in three mammalian lineages, but the central evolutionary claim — that ketogenesis is not a universal precondition for the evolution of large mammalian brains — rests on incomplete evidence, as no direct metabolic data are presented and key inferences about temporal ordering and alternative metabolic strategies remain speculative.

**Summary**

Jebb and Hiller investigate the evolution of HMGCS2, the gene encoding the rate-limiting mitochondrial HMG-CoA synthase required for ketone body biosynthesis, across 70 placental mammals. Using a combination of whole-genome alignment inspection, unassembled read validation, molecular dating, and selection analyses, they report that HMGCS2 has been independently lost in cetaceans, pteropodid (Old World fruit) bats, and Elephantimorpha (elephants and mastodons). They further argue that because species in all three lineages have relatively large brains, and because molecular dating suggests that gene loss preceded brain expansion in cetaceans and elephants, ketogenesis is not a universal precondition for the evolution of large mammalian brains. The downstream ketogenesis gene BDH1 is also lost in cetaceans and pteropodids, while pleiotropic pathway genes (ACAT1, HMGCL) remain intact, consistent with selective loss of non-pleiotropic ketogenesis components. The paper concludes by highlighting unexpected flexibility in mammalian energy metabolism and suggesting that alternative brain-fueling strategies must have evolved independently at least twice.

**Strengths**

- The genomic evidence for HMGCS2 loss in all three lineages is thoroughly documented and rigorously validated. Shared inactivating mutations (e.g., shared exon 1 deletion breakpoints in toothed and baleen whales, shared 2 bp insertion in exon 2 between killer whale and minke whale) provide strong parsimony-based evidence for ancestral gene inactivation. All 22 cetacean mutations are confirmed by ≥30 unassembled sequencing reads with zero support for the ancestral allele, and the Egyptian fruit bat deletion is independently validated with a PacBio assembly — these represent best practices in comparative genomics.

- The survey of the Elephantimorpha lineage is particularly impressive: negative blastn results for deleted HMGCS2 exons across two savanna elephant individuals, African forest elephant, Asian elephant, and two mastodon specimens, with positive controls (remaining HMGCS2 exons and all HMGCS1 exons) found in every species, provides compelling evidence that the deletion predates the mastodon–elephant divergence (~28–24 Mya).

- The analysis of purifying selection (dN/dS = 0.16 across 59 non-loss species) effectively contextualizes the rarity and significance of the loss events. The RELAX analyses confirming relaxed selection in all three loss lineages (p < 3×10⁻³) complement the structural evidence for pseudogenization.

- The investigation of whether HMGCS1 could compensate for HMGCS2 loss is a necessary and well-executed analysis. The TargetP results showing no mitochondrial targeting peptide, combined with Augustus gene predictions and liver RNA-seq examination in two species, address the most obvious alternative hypothesis.

- The analysis of downstream pathway genes adds valuable biological context: the loss of BDH1 (non-pleiotropic) but retention of ACAT1 and HMGCL (pleiotropic) in cetaceans and pteropodids is consistent with the expected molecular decay pattern following loss of a pathway's rate-limiting step.

- The authors commendably acknowledge that the pteropodid case does not support the temporal ordering argument (brain expansion likely preceded HMGCS2 loss), demonstrating intellectual honesty about the limits of their data.

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**

- **The claim that HMGCS2 loss results in "complete loss of ketogenesis" (a key inference underlying the entire narrative) is not directly demonstrated.** No metabolic measurements of ketone body levels are presented by the authors for any of the three lineages. The cited dolphin fasting study (Ridgway, 2013) is from a single species, and no metabolic data exist for elephants. The paper should more carefully distinguish between "loss of the gene encoding the rate-limiting enzyme" and "complete loss of the metabolic pathway," and should explicitly discuss the possibility — however unlikely — that non-canonical or currently uncharacterized pathways could contribute to residual ketone body production.

- **The central claim that "ketogenesis is not a universal precondition for the evolution of large mammalian brains" conflates necessity with importance.** Showing that two lineages expanded brain size after HMGCS2 loss demonstrates that ketogenesis is not strictly *necessary* for brain expansion, but does not address whether it was important or facilitative. The title and framing should be moderated to reflect this logical distinction. Additionally, the association between HMGCS2 loss and large brain size is observed in only three lineages — many small-brained species retain HMGCS2, and the correlation could be coincidental. A more rigorous statistical framework (e.g., phylogenetic comparative methods testing for association between gene loss and brain size) would strengthen this claim.

- **The temporal ordering of gene loss relative to brain expansion is uncertain, particularly for the elephant lineage.** The molecular dating estimate for HMGCS2 loss (45–42 Mya) and the estimated onset of brain expansion (~37 Mya) are separated by only ~5–8 million years, which is within the combined uncertainty of both estimates. The molecular dating itself depends on the assumption that synonymous rates in functional genes are 70% of pseudogene rates — this assumption should be justified with sensitivity analyses showing how the dating changes under alternative rate ratios (e.g., 60% or 80%). Without this, the claim that "loss preceded brain expansion" in elephants cannot be confidently distinguished from simultaneous or overlapping timing.

- **The claim that "alternative metabolic strategies to fuel large brains during fasting have evolved at least twice independently" is asserted but not supported by new data.** For dolphins, the paper cites one published study (Ridgway, 2013); for elephants, literally no data on fasting metabolism or brain fueling are presented. The musth/blood pH observation (Rasmussen and Perrin, 1999) — the only elephant-specific evidence cited — is evaluated as low-support in the DAG analysis because blood alkalinity during musth could have many causes unrelated to ketone body production (hyperventilation, hormonal changes, altered kidney function). This claim should be substantially tempered or removed.

- **The retention of BDH1 in the elephant lineage partially breaks the expected pattern of correlated gene loss and is not adequately explained.** If HMGCS2 loss eliminates ketogenesis, BDH1 (which acts downstream) should also experience relaxed selection, as observed in cetaceans and pteropodids. The elephant exception could indicate that BDH1 has additional, unrecognized functions, which would complicate the interpretation of BDH1 loss in the other lineages as being causally related to HMGCS2 loss. The authors should discuss this discrepancy more thoroughly.

- **No elephant liver RNA-seq data were examined**, leaving a gap in the HMGCS1 compensation analysis for one of the three lineages. The authors should acknowledge this limitation explicitly and discuss whether condition-specific (e.g., fasting-induced) alternative splicing of HMGCS1 could be missed by the available RNA-seq datasets, which were generated under unspecified conditions.

**Minor Comments**

- The paper would benefit from reporting confidence intervals or branch-specific variation in dN/dS among the 59 non-loss species, as the average dN/dS of 0.16 may mask lineage-specific variation that could suggest incipient relaxation of selection in other clades.

- The exclusion of two species (proboscis monkey and lesser Egyptian jerboa) due to assembly gaps is appropriately noted but should be briefly discussed in terms of whether these species might represent additional loss events.

- The BDH1 loss in shrews (which retain intact HMGCS2) is an interesting observation that deserves slightly more discussion, as it demonstrates that BDH1 loss can occur independently of HMGCS2 loss and complicates the correlated loss narrative.

- The connection drawn between cognitive behaviors (tool use, self-recognition, vocal learning) and brain metabolic fuel requirements is not established and reads as tangential to the core argument. Consider reducing this section or clarifying its relevance.

- The conservation implications for pteropodid bats, while interesting, are speculative and should be clearly flagged as such.

- Figure 1B is information-dense; a supplementary table summarizing all mutations, their genomic coordinates, and read support counts would improve accessibility.

**Recommendation**

Major Revisions

The genomic evidence for HMGCS2 loss is compelling (35 of 47 claims receive high support), but several central interpretive claims — particularly regarding the completeness of ketogenesis loss, the temporal ordering of gene loss relative to brain expansion in elephants, and the existence of alternative metabolic strategies — require either additional evidence (metabolic data, sensitivity analyses for molecular dating) or substantial moderation of language to meet eLife's standards for a paper making broad evolutionary claims about mammalian brain metabolism.
