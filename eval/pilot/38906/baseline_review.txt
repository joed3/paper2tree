**eLife Assessment**
This valuable study provides convincing genomic evidence for the independent loss of HMGCS2 in three mammalian lineages, though the broader evolutionary claims connecting ketogenesis loss to brain size evolution rest on incomplete evidence and would benefit from additional metabolic data and more cautious interpretation.

**Summary**
The authors investigate the evolution of HMGCS2, the gene encoding the rate-limiting enzyme of ketogenesis, across 70+ placental mammals using whole-genome alignments. They discover that HMGCS2 has been independently lost through large exon deletions and accumulation of inactivating mutations in cetaceans (dolphins and whales), pteropodid bats (Old World fruit bats), and Elephantimorpha (elephants and mastodons). Each loss is validated using unassembled sequencing reads, and additional analyses confirm relaxed selection on remaining gene remnants, loss of the non-pleiotropic downstream gene BDH1 in cetaceans and pteropodids, and the absence of compensatory mitochondrial targeting of the paralog HMGCS1. The authors argue that because all three lineages possess relatively large brains, ketone bodies are not universally required for fueling complex mammalian brains, and they use molecular dating to suggest that HMGCS2 loss preceded brain size expansion in cetaceans and elephants—challenging the hypothesis that ketogenesis was a universal precondition for brain enlargement.

**Strengths**
- The genomic evidence for HMGCS2 loss is thorough and multi-layered: exon deletions with shared breakpoints, frameshifting indels, splice site disruptions, promoter deletions, and relaxed selection signatures are documented across all three lineages. The use of unassembled reads to validate every mutation (≥30 reads, no ancestral allele support) is rigorous.
- The investigation extends beyond the focal species to include extinct mastodons, African forest elephants, and Asian elephants, strengthening the inference that the deletion predates the Elephantimorpha divergence (~28–24 Mya). This creative use of ancient and cross-species read data adds depth.
- The analysis of pathway genes (ACAT1, HMGCL, BDH1) and the HMGCS1 paralog is well-conceived, demonstrating that only non-pleiotropic ketogenesis genes are co-lost and that no compensatory mitochondrial targeting of HMGCS1 exists.
- The finding itself is conceptually striking—three independent losses of a gene considered essential for surviving fasting in mammals—and challenges widely held assumptions about the metabolic prerequisites for brain expansion.
- The paper is clearly written and the figures, particularly the genome browser visualizations with read-level validation insets, are effective and transparent.

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**
- **No direct metabolic evidence for loss of ketogenesis.** The entire argument that ketogenesis is abolished rests on genomic inference. While the Ridgway (2013) dolphin fasting study is cited, no original metabolic data (blood ketone body measurements, liver metabolomics) are presented for any species. For elephants and fruit bats, the evidence is particularly thin—the elephant argument relies on a single observation about blood pH during musth (Rasmussen and Perrin, 1999), and the fruit bat claim on a decades-old captive mortality report (van der Westhuyzen, 1978). The authors should either generate or more systematically compile metabolic data, or significantly temper the claim that "ketogenesis is lost."
- **The brain size/ketogenesis evolutionary narrative is underdeveloped and potentially overinterpreted.** The central conceptual claim—that ketogenesis is not a prerequisite for brain expansion—rests on molecular dating of gene loss events and paleontological EQ estimates, both of which carry large uncertainties. The molecular dating method assumes a fixed synonymous rate ratio (70%) and the confidence intervals are wide (e.g., 50–47 Mya for cetaceans, 45–42 Mya for elephants). The EQ values for ancestral species are based on limited fossil evidence. The authors should present sensitivity analyses for the dating method and more explicitly acknowledge the uncertainty in timing relative to brain expansion.
- **Alternative compensatory metabolic pathways are not investigated.** The paper dismisses HMGCS1 compensation but does not explore whether other metabolic adaptations (e.g., enhanced gluconeogenesis, modified fatty acid oxidation, or changes in glucose transporter expression) have evolved in these lineages. A comparative genomic scan for positively selected genes in gluconeogenesis or glucose metabolism pathways would substantially strengthen the manuscript.
- **Taxonomic sampling within each clade is limited.** Only 4 cetacean, 3 pteropodid, and 1 elephant genome assembly are examined in detail. The inclusion of additional species (e.g., more baleen whales, river dolphins, or other pteropodid genera) would refine the timing of loss events and strengthen phylogenetic inference. The authors should discuss whether additional genome assemblies now available could be incorporated.
- **The conservation implications for fruit bats are speculative.** The claim that HMGCS2 loss "may now represent a disadvantage" and is relevant to conservation is based on a single old study of captive fasting mortality. This point requires either stronger supporting evidence or should be presented more cautiously.

**Minor Comments**
- The paper would benefit from a supplementary table summarizing key life history traits (diet, fasting behavior, body mass, brain mass, EQ) for all species analyzed, to make the ecological context more accessible.
- The heterozygous 1 bp deletion in the black flying fox is noted as consistent with "ongoing gene erosion," but the possibility that this represents a sequencing artifact or somatic mutation should be briefly discussed.
- The discussion of BDH1 loss in shrews (Figure 1—figure supplement 5) feels tangential and somewhat disconnected from the main narrative. Consider moving this to supplementary material or integrating it more explicitly.
- Figure 2 panels A–C could be improved by including explicit confidence intervals on the molecular dating estimates directly on the phylogenies.
- The statement that HMGCS2 deficiency in humans is "probably underdiagnosed" is interesting but unsupported by the cited references; consider softening or providing additional support.
- Consider discussing whether marine mammals' high-fat, high-protein diets might provide sufficient glucogenic substrates to make ketogenesis dispensable, as this ecological context is important for interpreting the cetacean loss.

**Recommendation**
Minor Revisions
The core genomic finding is novel and well-supported, meeting eLife's standards for a conceptual advance, but the broader evolutionary claims regarding brain size require more careful framing of uncertainties, and the absence of any metabolic validation—even compiled from existing literature—is a notable gap that should be addressed.
