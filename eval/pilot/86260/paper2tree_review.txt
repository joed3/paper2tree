**eLife Assessment**

This study presents a **useful** characterization of metabolic alterations in endothelial cells derived from acute myocardial infarction thrombectomy specimens, but the evidence supporting the central mechanistic claims is **incomplete**. While descriptive metabolic profiling is internally consistent across multiple patient-derived lines, the absence of flux measurements, isotope tracing, perturbation experiments, and appropriate controls prevents the core claims of metabolic reprogramming causality and pharmacological actionability from being substantiated.

**Summary**

This paper introduces a novel cell model system — endothelial cells isolated from coronary thrombectomy specimens of acute myocardial infarction (AMI) patients — and characterizes their functional and metabolic phenotypes relative to a commercial human coronary artery endothelial cell (HCAEC) line. The authors report that AMI-derived ECs (AMIECs) display impaired proliferation, migration, and tubulogenesis, accompanied by a constellation of metabolic alterations: reduced glucose consumption with PFKFB3 downregulation, elevated lactate production, increased glutamine consumption with GLS1 upregulation, mitochondrial hyperpolarization, elevated ROS, and compensatory glutathione and pentose phosphate pathway activation (via G6PD and PFKFB4 upregulation). The authors synthesize these observations into a model proposing two "primary cell-autonomous deficiencies" — mitochondrial hyperpolarization driving excess ROS, and a glucose-to-glutamine carbon source switch — and suggest that pharmacological mitochondrial uncoupling could represent a therapeutic strategy.

The experimental approach combines functional assays (growth curves, wound healing, Matrigel tubulogenesis), protein expression analysis (Western blotting for metabolic enzymes), metabolite measurements (spectrophotometry for glucose/lactate/glutamine in media; GC/MS for intracellular amino acids), flow cytometry (cell cycle, ROS, mitochondrial membrane potential), and enzyme activity assays (G6PD). While the breadth of characterization is commendable, the study relies almost entirely on indirect proxies for metabolic flux and lacks the mechanistic and causal experiments needed to support its ambitious interpretive framework.

**Strengths**

- **Novel cell model system**: The derivation of endothelial cells from AMI thrombectomy specimens is a genuinely original approach that could enable patient-specific studies of coronary endothelial dysfunction. Eight independent patient-derived lines provide meaningful biological replication, and the consistent identification of phenotypic patterns across these lines strengthens confidence in the observations.

- **Mitochondrial membrane potential measurements** (DiIC1(5) data): All 8 AMIEC lines show significantly higher mitochondrial membrane potential than controls, with appropriate CCCP depolarization controls confirming signal specificity. This is the most universal and robust finding in the paper (high support).

- **ROS and glutathione measurements**: Seven of 8 AMIECs show significantly elevated ROS (H2DCFDA), and all 8 show elevated reduced glutathione content normalized to protein. The co-occurrence of high ROS with high GSH is biologically coherent as a compensatory response, and the internal exception (CE-45, with near-normal ROS and near-normal G6PD) strengthens the overall pattern (high support).

- **G6PD expression and activity concordance**: Both protein expression (Western blotting) and enzyme activity were measured for G6PD, with concordant results showing upregulation in AMIECs. This dual measurement approach is more rigorous than expression data alone (high support).

- **PFKFB3 downregulation and its correlation with glucose consumption**: PFKFB3 protein levels are consistently lower in 7/8 AMIECs, and CE-45 (with near-normal PFKFB3 and higher glucose consumption) serves as an informative internal reference point reinforcing the PFKFB3-glucose consumption association (high support).

- **GLS1 isoform characterization**: Use of isoform-specific antibodies to distinguish KGA and GAC, with consistent upregulation of both isoforms across patient lines, provides solid evidence for enhanced glutaminolytic enzyme expression (high support).

- **Intracellular amino acid profiling by GC/MS**: Elevated cysteine and glutamate — both GSH precursors — across all AMIECs, measured by a gold-standard analytical method with internal standard normalization, provides strong correlative evidence consistent with enhanced glutathione biosynthesis (high support).

- **Migration assays with proliferation control**: The use of mitomycin C to isolate migration from proliferation in wound-healing assays demonstrates methodological rigor, and consistent impairment across all 8 lines with statistical significance supports genuine migratory dysfunction (high support).

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**

- **Single control cell line is a critical design flaw**: The entire study compares 8 patient-derived lines against a single commercial HCAEC line. This makes it fundamentally impossible to distinguish disease-specific metabolic reprogramming from inter-individual variation in healthy coronary endothelial cell metabolism. At minimum, multiple healthy donor-derived coronary EC lines (ideally age- and sex-matched) are required. Without this, the central claim of "disease-associated metabolic dysfunction" versus "normal biological variation" cannot be evaluated. This weakness undermines virtually every claim in the paper.

- **The glucose-to-glutamine carbon source switch claim has low support**: The assertion that AMIECs "mainly resort to glutamine as their carbon source" (PRIMARY claim, low support) is made without any isotope tracing experiments (¹³C-glutamine or ¹³C-glucose). No direct measurement of TCA cycle flux from glutamine-derived carbons was performed, no malic enzyme expression or activity was measured, and no oxygen consumption rate data were collected. The leap from consumption ratios and enzyme expression to a "carbon source switch" requires carbon tracing data — this is the single most critical missing experiment.

- **Glutamine-derived lactate production is entirely speculative** (SUPPORTING claim, low support): The proposed pathway (glutamine → glutamate → α-KG → malate → pyruvate → lactate via malic enzyme) is invoked from pancreatic cancer cell literature without any direct evidence in endothelial cells. No ¹³C-glutamine tracing, no malic enzyme measurements, and no LDH-A data are provided. This mechanistic assertion is central to the paper's model yet lacks any experimental support.

- **"Pharmacologically actionable" dysfunction is untested** (PRIMARY claim, low support): The root claim describes the metabolic dysfunction as "pharmacologically actionable," and a supporting claim proposes mitochondrial uncouplers as therapeutic agents. Yet no pharmacological intervention, no rescue experiment, and no genetic perturbation of any kind was performed in this study. The sole evidence cited is an animal model study (Gu et al., 2021) of UCP1 knockout, which addresses a fundamentally different biological context. This claim should either be removed or clearly labeled as untested speculation.

- **No direct measurement of mitochondrial respiration**: The claim that AMIECs show "increased mitochondrial activity" (PRIMARY, medium support) relies solely on DiIC1(5) membrane potential measurements without Seahorse OCR data, MitoSOX for mitochondrial-specific ROS, or mitochondrial mass normalization (e.g., MitoTracker Green, citrate synthase). High membrane potential could reflect impaired ATP synthase function or reduced ATP demand rather than increased respiratory activity. This alternative was not experimentally addressed.

- **No causal or perturbation experiments**: The designation of metabolic changes as "primary cell-autonomous deficiencies" implies causality, but the study is entirely correlative. No PFKFB3 overexpression, GLS1 knockdown, uncoupler treatment, antioxidant rescue, or any other perturbation was performed. The paper cannot distinguish primary deficiencies from secondary adaptations or compensatory responses. The authors should either perform at least one key rescue experiment (e.g., PFKFB3 overexpression to test rescue of migration/tubulogenesis; mitochondrial uncoupler to test ROS reduction) or substantially temper the causal language throughout the manuscript.

- **PPP flux is inferred but not measured** (PRIMARY claim, medium support): The "glycolysis-to-PPP shunt" is based on G6PD expression/activity and PFKFB4 upregulation, but no direct PPP flux measurement (e.g., ¹⁴C-glucose oxidation ratio, ¹³C-glucose tracing into PPP products), NADPH quantification, or ribose-5-phosphate levels were obtained. The mechanistic link between PFKFB4 and PPP shunting is extrapolated from cancer cell literature and has not been validated in endothelial cells.

- **Blunted p53 as a driver of PFKFB4 upregulation is unsupported** (SUPPORTING claim, low support): No p53 expression, mutation status, or target gene analysis was performed. This speculation should be clearly demarcated as a hypothesis requiring future investigation rather than presented as a mechanistic explanation.

- **Normalization and technical concerns**: Throughout the paper, it is unclear whether metabolite consumption/production data are normalized to cell number — this is critical given the substantial growth rate differences between AMIECs and HCAECs. Similarly, DiIC1(5) data are not normalized for mitochondrial mass. The mitomycin C concentration reported (1 mg/mL) appears extraordinarily high compared to standard usage (5–10 μg/mL); this should be clarified and, if accurate, justified with viability data demonstrating no differential toxicity.

**Minor Comments**

- The figure legend for Western blotting of endothelial markers mentions "CD-10" rather than "CD-105" — this should be corrected if it is a typographical error.

- A sentence about aspartate's role in NAD⁺ and NADPH production appears to be duplicated in the text, suggesting an editing oversight.

- Figure 5A legend references "Glutamine and glucose production levels" — glucose consumption should presumably be described as consumption rather than production.

- The R² = 0.67 correlation between migration and tubulogenesis (9 data points, with the control as a clear leverage point) should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Regression diagnostics (p-value, confidence interval, Cook's distance for the control point) should be reported, and the correlation should be assessed with the control removed to determine whether it is driven by this single leverage point.

- The paper would benefit from a supplementary table summarizing patient demographics, comorbidities, medications, and AMI characteristics, as these could influence the metabolic phenotype of derived cells.

- The passage numbers at which AMIECs and HCAECs were used for each experiment should be clearly stated, as passage-dependent changes could confound comparisons.

- The claim that growth slowing "affects all phases of the cell cycle equally" based on a single 24-hr snapshot is over-interpreted; this should be softened or additional time points provided.

**Recommendation**

Major Revisions

While the paper presents an interesting cell model system and internally consistent descriptive observations (28/52 claims with high support), the 6 low-support claims include two primary mechanistic assertions (glutamine carbon source switch, pharmacological actionability) that are central to the paper's thesis, and the absence of flux measurements, perturbation experiments, and appropriate multi-donor controls prevents the ambitious mechanistic framework from meeting eLife's standard for convincing evidence supporting claims of conceptual advance.
