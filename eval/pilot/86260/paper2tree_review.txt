**eLife Assessment**

This useful study presents an interesting patient-derived endothelial cell model from AMI thrombectomy specimens and provides solid evidence for phenotypic dysfunction (impaired proliferation, migration, tubulogenesis), but the central mechanistic claims regarding metabolic reprogramming — particularly the proposed glycolysis-to-glutaminolysis switch and pharmacologically actionable mitochondrial dysfunction — rest on incomplete evidence and require isotope tracing, direct respirometry, and perturbation experiments to substantiate.

**Summary**

This paper describes the isolation and characterization of endothelial cells from coronary thrombectomy specimens of eight acute myocardial infarction (AMI) patients. The authors report that these AMI-derived endothelial cells (AMIECs) are dysfunctional in proliferation, migration, and tubulogenesis compared to a commercial human coronary artery endothelial cell (HCAEC) control line. Metabolic characterization reveals lower glucose consumption and PFKFB3 expression in most AMIECs, higher glutamine consumption and GLS1 expression, elevated mitochondrial membrane potential, increased ROS, and upregulated glutathione — leading the authors to propose a model in which AMIECs undergo a cell-autonomous shift from glycolysis toward glutaminolysis, with mitochondrial hyperpolarization driving excess ROS that is countered by PPP-derived NADPH and glutamine-derived glutathione. The authors conclude that this metabolic dysfunction is "pharmacologically actionable" through mitochondrial uncoupling.

The experimental strategy relies on comparing eight patient-derived cell lines against a single commercial control line across a battery of phenotypic assays (growth curves, wound healing, tubulogenesis), metabolite measurements (glucose, lactate, glutamine, glutamate in media; intracellular amino acids by GC/MS), protein expression analyses (Western blotting for PFKFB3, PFKFB4, G6PD, GLS1 isoforms), enzyme activity (G6PD), and flow cytometry (cell cycle, ROS, mitochondrial membrane potential). While the breadth of characterization is commendable, the study lacks the direct flux measurements, respirometry, and perturbation experiments needed to support its central mechanistic claims.

**Strengths**

- The generation of endothelial cells from thrombectomy specimens of eight independent AMI patients represents a genuinely novel and clinically relevant cell model. The consistency of phenotypic observations (impaired growth, migration, tubulogenesis) across all eight patient-derived lines strengthens confidence that these are robust, reproducible phenotypes rather than artifacts of individual cultures.

- Endothelial identity is confirmed using four canonical markers (V-CAM1, PECAM-1, CD-34, CD-105), and functional impairment in wound healing is demonstrated using mitomycin C to specifically isolate migration from proliferation effects (high-support claims for migration and tubulogenesis assays).

- The finding that PFKFB3 is downregulated in 7/8 AMIECs, with the notable exception of CE-45 (which also shows near-normal glucose consumption and ROS levels), provides an internally consistent molecular correlate for the observed glycolytic deficit. The reciprocal upregulation of PFKFB4 is an interesting observation that warrants further investigation.

- The measurement of both G6PD protein expression and enzyme activity provides concordant evidence for enhanced oxidative PPP activity, which is more rigorous than relying on expression data alone.

- The universal finding that all 8 AMIECs exhibit elevated mitochondrial membrane potential (DiIC1(5), with appropriate CCCP controls) and elevated intracellular reduced glutathione represents the most consistent observation in the study and suggests a genuine metabolic alteration in these cells.

- GC/MS-based intracellular amino acid profiling, showing elevated cysteine and glutamate (both GSH precursors) in AMIECs, adds an orthogonal layer of evidence supporting enhanced glutathione biosynthesis.

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**

- **Single control line is a critical limitation.** The entire study compares eight patient-derived lines against a single commercial HCAEC line. Without multiple healthy donor-derived coronary endothelial cell controls, it is impossible to distinguish disease-specific metabolic alterations from normal inter-individual variation or batch effects inherent to commercial cell lines. At minimum, 3–5 independent healthy coronary EC controls are needed. The authors must address this limitation explicitly and, ideally, include additional controls.

- **The central claim of a glycolysis-to-glutaminolysis carbon source switch (rated low support) is not substantiated by direct evidence.** No isotope tracing experiments (e.g., ¹³C-glucose or ¹³C-glutamine) were performed to demonstrate that glutamine carbons enter the TCA cycle, contribute to lactate production via malic enzyme, or serve as the primary carbon source. Glucose consumption from media is a crude proxy that does not distinguish glycolysis from PPP or other glucose-utilizing pathways. This is the single most critical missing experiment and must be addressed before the metabolic model can be considered validated.

- **No direct measurement of mitochondrial respiration was performed.** The claim that AMIECs have "increased mitochondrial activity" (medium support) is based solely on DiIC1(5) membrane potential measurements. Seahorse extracellular flux analysis (oxygen consumption rate) is essential to confirm or refute this claim. Notably, high membrane potential could reflect impaired ATP synthase function or reduced ATP demand rather than high respiratory activity — an alternative interpretation the authors do not adequately consider.

- **DiIC1(5) measurements are not normalized for mitochondrial mass.** Differences in mitochondrial content between AMIECs and HCAECs could confound membrane potential readings. Co-staining with a mass-dependent dye (e.g., MitoTracker Green) or measurement of citrate synthase activity is required to validate this finding.

- **The claim that the metabolic dysfunction is "pharmacologically actionable" (low support) is entirely speculative.** No pharmacological uncoupler was tested on AMIECs, and no rescue experiment of any kind (genetic or pharmacological) was performed. This claim should be removed from the abstract and title framing or explicitly flagged as a hypothesis for future investigation.

- **No causal perturbation experiments were conducted.** The proposed model — that mitochondrial hyperpolarization drives ROS, which drives PPP activation and glutaminolysis — remains correlative. Experiments such as PFKFB3 overexpression, GLS1 knockdown, or mitochondrial uncoupler treatment are needed to establish the directionality of the proposed metabolic cascade and distinguish primary deficiencies from secondary adaptations.

- **The explanation for high lactate production with low glucose consumption (medium support) invokes glutamine-derived lactate via malic enzyme, but no measurement of malic enzyme expression or activity was performed**, and no ¹³C-glutamine tracing into lactate was conducted. This remains an untested hypothesis that is presented with unwarranted certainty.

- **Normalization of metabolite consumption/production data is unclear.** Given the substantial differences in growth rates between AMIECs and HCAECs, it is essential that glucose, lactate, glutamine, and glutamate measurements from media are rigorously normalized to cell number at the time of sampling. The methods do not clearly describe this normalization, and growth rate differences over 48 hours could significantly confound these measurements.

- **The claim regarding blunted p53 function as a driver of PFKFB4 upregulation (low support) is presented without any supporting data** — no p53 expression, mutation status, or target gene analysis was performed. This should be clearly labeled as speculation.

**Minor Comments**

- Figure 5A legend refers to "Glutamine and glucose production levels" — glucose consumption should be reflected as negative values, but the labeling is potentially confusing and should be clarified.

- The mitomycin C concentration stated in the methods (1 mg/mL) appears extremely high relative to standard protocols (typically 1–10 µg/mL). The authors should verify this is correct and demonstrate that this concentration does not differentially affect AMIEC vs. HCAEC viability.

- Throughout the manuscript, "n=3" is stated for most experiments, but it is unclear whether these represent biological replicates (independent experiments from different passages) or technical replicates (triplicates within a single experiment). This distinction is critical for statistical interpretation and should be clarified.

- The correlation between migration and tubulogenesis (R²=0.67, 9 data points) should be interpreted cautiously, as the single HCAEC control point likely serves as a high-leverage point that inflates the correlation. Regression diagnostics (p-value, confidence intervals, Cook's distance) should be reported.

- The discussion of the Warburg effect in the context of CE-45 and CE-61 is interesting but somewhat tangential. The authors should consider whether these two outlier lines may represent a distinct subtype of endothelial dysfunction rather than forcing them into the same mechanistic framework.

- Western blot quantifications should ideally be validated with a second loading control beyond β-actin, or with total protein staining (e.g., Ponceau S), given potential variability in actin expression between cell types.

- The paper would benefit from a summary figure illustrating the proposed metabolic model with clear indication of which steps are directly measured, which are inferred, and which are speculative.

**Recommendation**

Major Revisions

While the patient-derived cell model is novel and the phenotypic characterization is solid, 6 of 52 claims receive low support and the two most central mechanistic claims — the glycolysis-to-glutaminolysis switch and the pharmacologically actionable nature of the dysfunction — lack the direct experimental evidence (isotope tracing, respirometry, perturbation experiments, additional controls) required to meet eLife's standard for convincing mechanistic insight. The paper in its current form presents a descriptive characterization that overreaches in its mechanistic conclusions.
