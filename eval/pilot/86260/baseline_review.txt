**eLife Assessment**
This potentially useful study describes metabolic alterations in coronary endothelial cells isolated from acute myocardial infarction patients, but the central claims regarding cell-autonomous metabolic reprogramming and its mechanistic link to endothelial dysfunction rest on incomplete evidence due to the lack of appropriate controls, absence of direct metabolic flux measurements, and a largely correlative analytical framework.

**Summary**
The authors isolate and culture coronary endothelial cells from thrombectomy specimens of eight STEMI patients (AMIECs) and compare them to a commercial human coronary artery endothelial cell line (HCAEC). They report that AMIECs exhibit reduced proliferation, impaired migration, and diminished tubulogenic capacity. Metabolically, AMIECs show reduced glucose consumption with maintained or increased lactate production, downregulation of PFKFB3 with upregulation of PFKFB4, increased G6PD expression and activity, elevated ROS, increased glutathione, enhanced glutamine consumption and glutaminase expression, and mitochondrial hyperpolarization. The authors propose a model in which AMIECs undergo a metabolic shift from glycolysis toward the pentose phosphate pathway and glutaminolysis, driven by mitochondrial dysfunction that produces excess ROS, with the metabolic reprogramming representing a compensatory mechanism to maintain redox homeostasis. They suggest this dysfunction may be pharmacologically targetable via mitochondrial uncouplers.

**Strengths**
- The use of patient-derived coronary endothelial cells from thrombectomy specimens is a creative and clinically relevant approach that provides a unique cell model for studying endothelial dysfunction in AMI, complementing animal models and population-level patient studies.
- The study is comprehensive in scope, examining proliferation, migration, tubulogenesis, glucose/glutamine metabolism, enzyme expression, ROS, glutathione, amino acid profiling, and mitochondrial membrane potential across eight independent patient-derived cell lines.
- The correlation between migration defects and tubulogenesis impairment (R²=0.67) across patient lines provides a coherent functional link between these phenotypes.
- The consistent directionality of most metabolic alterations across the majority of patient lines (e.g., 7/8 for PFKFB3 downregulation, 7/8 for elevated ROS) lends some robustness to the observations despite heterogeneity.
- The proposed integrative metabolic model connecting mitochondrial hyperpolarization, ROS overproduction, PPP shunting, and glutaminolysis-dependent glutathione synthesis is intellectually coherent and generates testable hypotheses.

**Weaknesses / Essential Revisions**

- **Inadequate controls represent a fundamental limitation.** The entire study compares eight patient-derived lines to a single commercial HCAEC line from Lonza. This design cannot distinguish AMI-related changes from donor-to-donor variability, differences in isolation methodology, passage history, or other confounders. At minimum, coronary endothelial cells from multiple healthy donors (or non-culprit coronary arteries from the same patients) processed identically should serve as controls. Without this, the attribution of any observed differences to AMI pathology is unsubstantiated.

- **The central metabolic claims are not supported by direct flux measurements.** The proposed glycolysis-to-PPP shunt is inferred from enzyme expression levels (PFKFB3/PFKFB4/G6PD) but never directly measured. ¹³C-glucose and ¹³C-glutamine isotope tracing experiments are essential to confirm (a) reduced glycolytic flux, (b) increased PPP flux, (c) glutamine as the source of excess lactate via malic enzyme, and (d) the anaplerotic contribution of glutamine to the TCA cycle. Without these, the metabolic model remains speculative.

- **Mitochondrial respiration is not directly measured.** The claim of increased mitochondrial activity rests solely on membrane potential (DiIC1(5) staining). Oxygen consumption rate measurements (e.g., Seahorse XF analysis) with appropriate inhibitors (oligomycin, FCCP, rotenone/antimycin A) are needed to assess basal respiration, ATP-linked respiration, maximal capacity, and spare respiratory capacity. Hyperpolarization alone can reflect multiple states, including impaired ATP synthase activity rather than increased electron transport.

- **No causal or rescue experiments are presented.** The paper claims "pharmacologically actionable" dysfunction but tests no pharmacological interventions. Key experiments would include: (a) treatment with mitochondrial uncouplers (e.g., BAM15, DNP) to test whether reducing membrane potential rescues ROS, metabolic profiles, and functional phenotypes; (b) glutaminase inhibition (e.g., CB-839) to assess dependence on glutaminolysis; (c) PFKFB3 activation or overexpression to test whether restoring glycolytic flux rescues migration and tubulogenesis.

- **Key intermediary measurements are missing.** No data are provided for fructose-2,6-bisphosphate levels (direct readout of PFKFB3/4 balance), NADPH/NADP⁺ ratios, NAD⁺/NADH ratios, LDH-A expression/activity, malic enzyme expression/activity, or pyruvate levels — all of which are central to the proposed model.

- **Statistical approach is problematic.** Individual Student's t-tests comparing each AMIEC line to the single HCAEC control, without correction for multiple comparisons, inflates type I error. The data structure (8 patient lines vs. 1 control) would be better served by treating AMIECs as a group with appropriate mixed-effects models or non-parametric tests, ideally with multiple control donors.

- **The n=3 throughout the manuscript is ambiguous.** It is unclear whether n=3 refers to biological replicates (independent experiments) or technical replicates. For patient-derived lines, the biological replicate is the patient (n=8 for AMIECs, n=1 for controls), and technical replicates within a single line do not add biological power. This needs explicit clarification.

- **Endothelial cell identity and purity require more rigorous validation.** Western blotting for four markers is insufficient. Flow cytometry for endothelial markers (CD31, CD144/VE-cadherin, vWF) and exclusion of contaminating cell types (smooth muscle cells: α-SMA; fibroblasts: FSP-1; leukocytes: CD45) would strengthen confidence in cell identity, particularly given the heterogeneous source material (thrombectomy specimens).

- **The claim of "cell-autonomous" metabolic dysfunction is overstated.** The authors acknowledge but inadequately address the possibility that observed phenotypes reflect epigenetic memory of the in vivo environment, culture-induced selection, or adaptation artifacts. Demonstrating persistence of phenotypes across multiple passages, or comparing early vs. late passage cells, would help distinguish intrinsic from acquired features.

- **Patient clinical heterogeneity is insufficiently addressed.** Table 1 is referenced but its contents are only partially described. Medications (statins, anti-platelet agents), comorbidities, and extent of coronary disease could profoundly influence endothelial cell phenotypes. No attempt is made to correlate metabolic features with clinical characteristics.

**Minor Comments**

- Figure panel labeling appears inconsistent with the legend descriptions (e.g., Figure 1 legend references panels A–G but the text refers to panels differently — e.g., "Figure 1D" for growth curves vs. "Figure 1E" for cell cycle). This should be carefully corrected throughout.
- The manuscript lacks a formal Discussion section structure; the discussion is interspersed throughout the Results, making it difficult to distinguish observations from interpretations.
- The tubulogenesis assay uses VEGF stimulation but the concentration is not specified.
- The term "AMIECs" is used interchangeably with "patient-derived cells" and "coronary endothelial cells" — consistent terminology would improve clarity.
- The claim that CE-45 behaves most like control cells is interesting but unexplored — do clinical features of this patient differ from others?
- Several key references for the metabolic model (e.g., glutamine-derived lactate via malic enzyme) cite reviews rather than primary data in endothelial cells, weakening the mechanistic inference.
- The writing would benefit from a clearer separation of established knowledge from novel observations, particularly in the metabolic interpretation sections.

**Recommendation**
Major Revisions
The study addresses a clinically important question with a novel cell model, but the absence of appropriate multi-donor controls, direct metabolic flux measurements, functional rescue experiments, and rigorous statistical analysis means the central claims are not adequately supported for publication in eLife, which requires that conclusions be firmly grounded in the presented evidence.
